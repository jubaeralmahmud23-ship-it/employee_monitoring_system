## Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Kept: papers that appeared in the results above, that address one of the scoped questions (judge validity or bias, contamination or leakage, reproducibility, statistical practice, sycophancy or honesty, agent prompt-injection security), and for which at least an abstract could be read from arXiv, the Semantic Scholar API, the arXiv API or the ACL Anthology. Preference was given to peer-reviewed venues (NeurIPS, ICLR, ACL, NAACL, EMNLP, USENIX Security, ICSE, AIES, ICML); preprints were kept when they were central to the area (Miller 2024 on error bars, Preference Leakage (its arXiv record now says accepted at ICLR 2026; see the citation audit), MASK, "The Attacker Moves Second", non-determinism) and are labelled as preprints.

Dropped: vendor and consultancy blogs (galileo.ai, adaline.ai, futureagi.com, promptlayer.com, zeroentropy.dev, medium.com, substack posts), aggregator or mirror pages (themoonlight.io, alphaxiv.org, liner.com, paperswithcode mirrors, sotaverified.org, hyper.ai, tldr.takara.ai), a ResearchGate-only systematic review that could not be opened, a medRxiv clinical-screening preprint (off topic), a USPTO patent, and PMC clinical papers (off scope for IS/CS). The single AIS eLibrary hit (HICSS-57, generative LLM limitations) was off topic and dropped. Papers only seen as snippets in domain-filtered searches (ACM DefensiveTokens, Springer agentic-evaluation review, ScienceDirect survey page) were not promoted to the evidence table because no abstract was read; they are mentioned in the search log only. Papers first published in 2026 with arXiv ids 2604 to 2609 were kept only when their abstract was read (Reliability without Validity); others are named in the log as leads.

## Synthesis

### Agreements

Across the judge-validity papers there is agreement that LLM judges can approximate human majority preference on easy, open-ended comparisons (Zheng et al. report 85% non-tie agreement, matching human-human agreement), but that this transfers poorly to harder or more specific properties: Bavaresco et al. find reliability varies with the property, annotator expertise and whether the text is model-generated; JudgeBench shows judges near chance on objectively verifiable pairs; CALM and Shi et al. find task- and judge-specific biases that persist in frontier models. The 2026 preprint "Reliability without Validity" quantifies what the earlier papers only hint at: exact-match agreement overstates chance-corrected agreement by 33-41 points and judge rankings do not transfer across benchmarks. Self-preference is treated as real in three independent records (Zheng's inconclusive observation, Wataoka's perplexity explanation, Panickssery's self-recognition link) and Preference Leakage extends it to generator-judge relatedness in training pipelines.

The contamination papers agree that contamination is widespread and hard to detect for closed models (Deng et al.; Xu et al.), and that it sits on a continuum with legitimate task-targeted training (Dominguez-Olmedo et al.). The Leaderboard Illusion adds a non-training channel: selective disclosure and unequal data access distort human-preference leaderboards without any test-set leakage.

The security papers agree that prompt injection succeeds at high rates (Combined Attack ASV 0.75 on GPT-4; ASB up to 84.30%; InjecAgent 24% for ReAct GPT-4), that larger or more instruction-following models are not safer (Liu et al. find a positive size correlation), and that defenses either fail or cost utility; Nasr et al. show that near-zero reported attack rates do not survive adaptive attackers.

The reproducibility and statistics papers agree that single-run point estimates are not adequate reporting: Atil et al. show up to 15% accuracy variation across nominally deterministic runs; the ICSE 2026 study reproduces none of five executable studies; Miller, Bowyer et al. and Lee et al. all call for explicit uncertainty quantification.

### Contradictions or tensions

1. Agreement metric. Zheng et al. and most follow-ups validate judges with raw agreement; "Reliability without Validity" and Lee et al. argue raw agreement is misleading and that calibration sets and chance correction are needed. These are not contradictory data but contradictory standards, and the field has not converged.
2. CLT error bars. Miller recommends CLT-based standard errors with clustering; Bowyer et al. argue CLT intervals are badly anti-conservative below a few hundred items, which covers JudgeBench (350 pairs), MT-bench (80 questions) and many domain evals. The two papers address different n and are compatible, but applied work rarely states which regime it is in.
3. Verbosity bias. Zheng et al. show a 91.3% failure rate for Claude-v1 and GPT-3.5 under a repetitive-list attack; the 2026 cohort study reports verbosity bias "small (< 0.011)" under a single pairwise rubric. Either the bias has shrunk in newer judges or the measurement designs differ; the sources received do not resolve it.
4. Self-preference as harm. Wataoka et al. and Panickssery et al. frame self-preference as bias; the 2025 preprint "Do LLM Evaluators Prefer Themselves for a Reason?" (seen only in a Semantic Scholar search abstract) asks whether it reflects genuinely better outputs on verifiable tasks. Preference Leakage sidesteps this by using related student models rather than the judge itself.
5. Position bias explanation. Zheng et al. speculate it is architectural or data-driven; Shi et al. find it is driven by the quality gap between candidates and only weakly by length, which suggests it is partly a tie-breaking behaviour rather than a fixed prior.
6. Honesty vs truthfulness. MASK argues truthfulness benchmarks measure accuracy, not honesty, so prior "honesty" results are not comparable with its own; this is a construct-validity dispute rather than conflicting measurements.

### Methodological weaknesses

- Validation of judges is usually against a single human majority label with ties removed and without inter-annotator reliability of the humans themselves beyond a headline number (visible in Zheng et al.; inferred for most abstracts).
- Few papers report run-to-run variance for judges; Atil et al. and the 2026 cohort study are exceptions. Temperature and model version are often not stated in abstracts, which the ICSE 2026 replication finds to be fatal for reproduction.
- Bias audits use synthetic perturbations (CALM, repetitive-list attack); whether perturbation-induced bias predicts bias on natural data is not shown in the sources received.
- Contamination detection for closed models relies on indirect probes (TS-Guessing) whose false-positive behaviour on strong reasoners is not characterised in the abstract.
- Security benchmarks are static or semi-static; Nasr et al. show that defense papers evaluated against static attack strings over-claim. AgentDojo's domains are consumer-style and do not include HR, monitoring or enterprise-document scenarios that an IS project would care about.
- Sycophancy evaluations (SycEval, Sharma et al.) measure generation under user pushback; none of the records received measures sycophancy of the judge toward the party that submitted the artefact being judged.
- IS venues are essentially absent from all domain-filtered searches (queries 9-14 and 24); the evaluation-methodology literature is CS-only as far as this scan reached.

### Unresolved questions

- Which validation protocol (chance-corrected agreement, calibration-set CIs, bias audit, test-retest) is sufficient for a judge to be used in a given decision context, and how much human-labelled calibration data does it need (Lee et al. give regimes, but for sensitivity/specificity of binary judgements only)?
- Do small open judges (1-4B, CPU-runnable) reach usable validity on any of the released human-judgement sets, or is validity concentrated in frontier models (Thakur et al. 2024, abstract seen in the batch: "only the best (and largest) models achieve reasonable alignment")?
- How large is preference leakage in realistic pipelines where generator and judge are the same open family (e.g. a Qwen or Llama judge grading Qwen or Llama outputs), and can it be detected without training students?
- What share of published LLM evaluations in IS and SE venues report any uncertainty, repeated runs, or judge validation? The ICSE 2026 study answers for reproducibility artefacts only.
- Are domain-specific indirect-injection benchmarks (NetInjectBench-style) transferable, and what is the injection risk in enterprise document pipelines such as resume screening (Liu et al.'s running example) or employee-communication summarisation?

## Candidate research gaps

- G1. Judge validation standards for domain tasks. Evidence: raw agreement is the dominant validation metric (Zheng et al. and most follow-ups), yet chance-corrected agreement is 33-41 points lower and rankings do not transfer across benchmarks (2026 cohort preprint); Bavaresco et al. show validity is property-specific; Lee et al. (ICML 2026) provide a correction that needs a calibration set. Gap: no evidence received on whether small open judges (CPU-runnable) pass a chance-corrected, calibrated validation on IS-style artefacts (process descriptions, requirements, decision rationales). Would be disproved by a paper that reports kappa and calibrated CIs for 1-4B open judges on released human-judgement sets (MT-bench, LLMBar, JUDGE-BENCH) or on IS artefacts.
- G2. Preference leakage and self-preference in same-family generate-and-judge pipelines. Evidence: Preference Leakage (ICLR 2026 per arXiv comment) shows judges favour related students; Wataoka et al. link the effect to perplexity; Panickssery et al. to self-recognition. Gap: no record received measures leakage when the judge is a small open model from the same family as the generator, which is exactly the setting a no-API project would use. Disproved by a leakage study across open families at small scale, or by evidence that perplexity-based familiarity is negligible below some model size.
- G3. Statistical and reporting practice audit for LLM evaluations in IS and BISE venues. Evidence: Miller, Bowyer et al. and Lee et al. give recommendations; Bean et al. audit 445 CS benchmarks for construct validity; the ICSE 2026 study audits SE reproducibility. Gap: no audit of IS venues was found (queries 9-14 and 24 returned no IS hits). Disproved by an existing IS meta-research paper auditing error bars, repeated runs, judge validation or model-version reporting; feasibility here is high because it needs no GPU.
- G4. Contamination and nondeterminism as threats to IS evaluation claims. Evidence: Deng et al. (TS-Guessing), Dominguez-Olmedo et al. (training on the test task), Atil et al. (up to 15% run variance), Leaderboard Illusion. Gap: no record applies contamination probes or N-run variance protocols to the business-process or enterprise datasets used in IS papers (BPI logs, requirements sets), so claims there are untested. Disproved by a study that runs TS-Guessing-style probes or repeated-run variance on such datasets.
- G5. Enterprise-document prompt injection at small scale. Evidence: Liu et al.'s resume-screening example, AgentDojo and ASB domains (email, banking, travel, e-commerce), NetInjectBench for network operations, Nasr et al. on adaptive attacks. Gap: no benchmark received covers HR, monitoring or internal-document pipelines, and none evaluates small open models as the defended backbone with adaptive attackers. Disproved by a domain benchmark covering enterprise documents or by evidence that AgentDojo results transfer unchanged.
- G6. Sycophancy of the judge toward the submitter. Evidence: Sharma et al. (preference data rewards agreement), SycEval (58% sycophancy, 78.5% persistence), CALM's authority and bandwagon biases, Chen et al.'s authority bias. Gap: none of the records received tests whether a judge's verdict shifts when the artefact's author asserts quality, seniority or a prior rating (AMEL and "Jagged Judges", seen as titles only, may address it). Disproved by reading those two 2026 preprints and finding the design already covered, or by a null result.
