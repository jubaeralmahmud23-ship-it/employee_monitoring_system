## Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Kept:
- Peer-reviewed papers and central preprints on how organizations govern, document, explain, audit or make compliant their AI systems, where the record appeared in a search result or API response received in this run and at least its abstract could be read (one exception, Wagner et al. 2026, is kept at metadata/snippet depth because the search snippet carried its ranked findings and the venue is a peer-reviewed software-engineering journal).
- Conceptual frameworks (Raji et al. 2020; Lam et al. 2024; Mäntymäki et al. 2022), interview and survey studies, a large-scale compliance field study (Wright et al. 2024), an online experiment (Kalff and Simbeck 2025), a platform-scale documentation analysis (Liang et al. 2024), a benchmark study (Kozodoi et al. 2022), systematic reviews, and a multiple case study, because each informs a different kind of gap.
- Both IS venues (Information Systems Frontiers, Information Systems Journal, ECIS, Information & Management) and CS/FAccT venues (FAT*/FAccT, AIES, CHI, PACM HCI, Nature Machine Intelligence) plus arXiv preprints labelled as such.

Dropped:
- Vendor blogs, consultancy surveys and press coverage (Superblocks, CRN, Alation, ModelOp, Cloud Security Alliance, PwC, McKinsey, TestDevLab, BABL AI marketing pages, Modulos, Kognitos, ComputerWeekly, The Register, Consumer Reports press release, HCLTech, Technology Magazine, verifywise, globaia). The Stanford AI Index 2026 and the HBR 2026 article were read only as snippets and are mentioned as context, not evidence.
- Technical fairness-auditing and explanation-fairness methods papers without an organizational or regulatory component (arXiv 2605.09852, 2408.02558, 2605.08671, 2207.01611, 2601.03087, NeurIPS 2023 "Auditing Fairness by Betting"), because the area brief targets governance and practice.
- Mirrors and secondary copies (alphaxiv, ar5iv, philarchive, paperswithcode, themoonlight, deepai, ResearchGate) when the primary record was available.
- Items whose titles only appeared in the result lists and that were not opened or resolved through an API (listed in the search log as "not opened"); they are not cited as evidence.
- A PhD thesis on arXiv (2606.18289) and two OpenReview PDFs of unclear review status are listed in the supplementary table only.

## Synthesis

### Agreements

- Across both the CS/FAccT and the IS literature read here, there is agreement that principles, frameworks and tools are plentiful while operational, validated practice is thin. Raji et al. (2020) already note that "the AI industry lacks proven methods to translate principles into practice"; Batool et al. find only 5 of 61 governance papers answer who/what/when/how; Kuehnert et al. find that over 220 RAI tools cluster on developers in modeling stages and are rarely validated; Berman et al. find that RAI-tool evaluations measure usability rather than effectiveness; Papagiannidis et al. and Heger et al. supply practice dimensions from three firms and 90 experts respectively but no effectiveness measurement.
- Auditing is the dominant accountability mechanism proposed, and every audit paper here agrees that an audit is only as good as its ecosystem: standards, auditor accreditation, disclosure and enforcement (Raji et al. 2020; Costanza-Chock et al. 2022; Raji et al. 2022; Lam et al. 2024; Schiff, Kelley and Camacho Ibáñez 2024). Schiff et al.'s 34 auditors report that audits lack external reporting and success measurement, which is exactly what Lam et al.'s criterion audit demands and what LL 144 does not require.
- The LL 144 studies converge: a transparency-driven regime with regulatee discretion and no outcome floor yields low observable compliance, probable publication bias in published impact ratios, and audits that are incomplete evaluations (Wright et al. 2024; Groves et al. 2024; Gerchick et al. 2025; Hilliard et al. 2024; Lam et al. 2024 section 5.3). Black et al. (2026) offer the contrasting case in which supervisory examinations, not transparency, drive decades of fair-lending compliance work.
- Explainability in business settings serves internal stakeholders and does not automatically produce understanding: Bhatt et al. (2020) find deployments serve engineers; Kalff and Simbeck (2025) find XAI raises HR managers' subjective trust without raising objective understanding; Ioannou et al. (2026) find explanation effects conditional on advisor type and risk; Van den Berg et al. (2023) find a wide set of social and technical aspects must be managed "by design".
- Documentation adoption is uneven and shallow on exactly the sections accountability needs (limitations, evaluation): Liang et al. (2024) at platform scale and Bhat et al. (2023) on public model cards agree.
- Operationalizing the EU AI Act is a "last mile" problem for teams and deployers: Jarvers and Papakyriakopoulos (team level), Wagner et al. (perceived difficulty highest for Art 10 data governance and Art 15 robustness), Lai et al. and Schrader et al. (missing traceability processes, maturity-dependent feasibility), and Tao et al. (automated checkers inadequate) all point the same way; Buscemi et al. and Paul and Nandy respond with mappings and executable criteria that are not yet independently evaluated.
- Fairness is treated as sociotechnical rather than metric-only by the IS theory paper (Dolata et al.), the IS accountability review (Kempton et al.) and the audit framework (Lam et al. add governance and risk criteria to the legally required disparate-impact analysis).

### Contradictions or tensions

- Internal versus external audits. Raji et al. argue internal audits are needed because only insiders have access to data, intermediate models and process, while admitting the shared organizational interest; Lam et al. and Raji et al. (2022) argue only independent external audits can provide assurance, yet external auditors depend on auditee documentation ("indirect verification") and face opinion shopping. Schiff et al.'s auditors describe audits that follow financial-audit stages but skip external reporting, so neither model is operating as its proponents describe.
- Audit requirement versus outcome requirement. LL 144 requires an audit but no passing threshold (Wright et al.; Lam et al. 5.3.1 and 5.3.3). Wright et al. argue a floor is needed even if "arbitrary and blunt"; Hilliard et al. argue outcome-only audits cannot prevent bias elsewhere in the tool; Black et al. show that supervision rather than any published threshold drove fair-lending practice. The literature does not agree on what the audited quantity should be.
- Explanation duties versus explanation effects. Regulation (AI Act Art 13, cited in the arXiv-filtered results) demands explanations sufficient to understand and contest decisions, but Kalff and Simbeck find explanations can reduce accurate understanding, and Bhatt et al. find explanations are built for engineers. Ioannou et al. find positive adoption effects in a financial-advice experiment, so the sign of the effect appears to depend on task, literacy and explanation style.
- Technical tractability versus organizational variation in credit fairness. Kozodoi et al. find discrimination can be reduced at low profit cost and several criteria satisfied at once, while Black et al. find that how institutions test and search for less discriminatory models "varies widely" and depends on business incentives and legal uncertainty. The constraint is organizational, not algorithmic.
- Automation of compliance. Paul and Nandy claim governance-as-code reproduces a manual audit on two deployments and cuts labor by about 75%; Tao et al. find twelve compliance checkers foster "a false sense of compliance"; Langer et al. argue human-oversight compliance resists checklists; Lin et al. warn that compliance data is not evaluation data. Jarvers and Papakyriakopoulos' practitioners already treat verification-oriented requirements as box-ticking.
- Documentation as evidence. Lam et al.'s indirect verification and Raji et al.'s design history file both rely on documentation produced by the audited party, while Liang et al. and Bhat et al. show that the limitation and evaluation sections most relevant to assurance are the least filled, and Liang et al.'s intervention suggests the incentive to document is download visibility.
- Governance structures. Hadley et al. find algorithm review boards impactful and leadership buy-in decisive; Kuehnert et al. find almost no tools built for leadership or deployers; Burnat and Davidson model why vendors stop at an observable procurement floor. Whether boards, roles or maturity dimensions change deployed-system outcomes is asserted, not shown.

### Methodological weaknesses

- Frameworks without empirical validation: Raji et al. (hypothetical cases), Lam et al. (practitioner experience inside a firm with a commercial stake in audits, no IRB), Mäntymäki et al. (conceptual), Buscemi et al. (mapping), Kim et al. (two cases, six interviews), Paul and Nandy (two deployments, authors' own benchmark).
- Small, self-selected qualitative samples drawn from an emerging community: Costanza-Chock et al. (survey N=152 of a catalog of 438), Schiff et al. (34), Groves et al. (16), Black et al. (35), Hadley et al. (17), Wei et al. (17), Papagiannidis et al. (three firms, one sector), Jarvers and Papakyriakopoulos (one startup, insider), Lai et al. (10 and 15).
- Compliance measurement cannot identify the denominator: Wright et al. can report a compliance rate but not a non-compliance rate; Gerchick et al. only see published audits; publication bias is plausible but unprovable with public data.
- Experiments on explanation use online samples, hypothetical tasks and single countries (Kalff and Simbeck, 410 German HR managers; Ioannou et al., online experiment), with no behavioral or downstream decision outcomes.
- Platform-scale documentation studies are single-platform and correlational (Liang et al.).
- Benchmark fairness work (Kozodoi et al.) is offline, dataset-dependent (datasets not named in the abstract) and limited to statistical criteria.
- Reviews have heterogeneous inclusion rules and small bases (Ribeiro et al., nine secondary studies; Batool et al., 61 articles; Bartsch et al. rely on the Web of Science IS category to define IS).
- Many 2026 items are preprints or early proceedings records with citation counts of 0-8 on Semantic Scholar; their findings have not been replicated or contested yet.
- For this scan: 13 of 16 main rows were read at abstract depth only, and one at metadata/snippet depth, so method details (sampling, measures) are inferred where marked.

### Unresolved questions

- Do mandated audits (internal or external) change the outcomes of deployed systems? The only outcome-level study surfaced is a seminar listing for a difference-in-differences study of LL 144 (not located as a paper); Wright et al. state that no one can currently know whether LL 144 reduces discrimination.
- What is the base rate of in-scope systems (AEDTs under LL 144, high-risk systems under the AI Act) across organizations? Every compliance study here lacks a denominator.
- Which organizational governance mechanisms (review boards, accountable roles, maturity dimensions, hourglass layers) have measurable effects on compliance or harm, and with what instruments would an IS study measure them?
- How do deployers, as opposed to providers, and small organizations operationalize AI Act obligations, and does automation help or produce false assurance?
- Does supervisory examination (fair lending) generalize to other domains and to the EU conformity-assessment regime?
- Which explanation designs improve objective understanding and contestability for HR and credit decision-makers, and how does AI literacy moderate this outside Germany and outside online experiments?
- Can audit or compliance documentation (model cards, audit reports, technical documentation) be trusted as assurance evidence, and how would an auditor detect misstatement?

## Candidate research gaps

- **Outcome evidence for audit mandates is missing.** Evidence that the gap exists: Wright et al., Groves et al., Gerchick et al. and Hilliard et al. document low observable compliance, incomplete audits and a probable publication bias under LL 144, and Wright et al. state explicitly that the law's effect on discrimination cannot be known; the forward-citation chase of the two LL 144 papers found no published causal outcome study. What would disprove it: a peer-reviewed study linking an audit mandate (LL 144, a state law, or an AI Act conformity assessment) to hiring, lending or incident outcomes with a credible counterfactual, for example the difference-in-differences paper listed only as a seminar in query 21 if it exists in published form.
- **No validated instrument measures organizational AI governance effectiveness.** Evidence: Kuehnert et al. (tools rarely validated), Berman et al. (evaluations measure usability, not effectiveness), Batool et al. (5 of 61 papers complete), Heger et al. (24 maturity dimensions without a measurement study), Papagiannidis et al. (three cases), Mäntymäki et al. (untested framework). What would disprove it: an IS paper (for example in MISQ, ISR, JAIS, ISF or ISJ) reporting a governance scale with predictive validity against audit findings, incidents or regulatory outcomes; this scan did not query those journals for "scale development", so a targeted search is the first check.
- **Deployer-side and SME operationalization of the EU AI Act is studied only at single-organization or perception level.** Evidence: Jarvers and Papakyriakopoulos (one startup), Wagner et al. (multiple case study of perceived difficulty; company size and maturity as factors), Lai et al. (10 interviews, 15 survey responses), Tao et al. (checkers give false assurance), Schrader et al. (feasibility varies with maturity). What would disprove it: a multi-organization, longitudinal study of deployers' compliance workflows after the August 2026 high-risk obligations, or evidence that harmonized standards have been adopted with measured effects.
- **Regulatory explanation duties lack field evidence that explanations improve decision-makers' objective understanding or contestability in HR and credit.** Evidence: Bhatt et al. (explanations serve engineers), Kalff and Simbeck (no objective gain, possible harm), Ioannou et al. (effects conditional on context), Van den Berg et al. (implementation complexity). What would disprove it: field experiments inside firms showing that a specific explanation design raises objective understanding or successful contestation by HR or credit staff or by affected individuals.
- **Documentation and audit reports are relied on as assurance evidence without evidence of their reliability.** Evidence: Lam et al.'s indirect verification and Raji et al.'s design history file depend on auditee documentation; Liang et al. and Bhat et al. show limitation and evaluation sections are least complete; Gerchick et al. show LL 144 reports omit demographics and use test data opaquely; Lin et al. argue compliance data is not evaluation data. What would disprove it: studies showing that documentation quality predicts audit findings or incidents, or audit protocols that detect misstatements in submitted documentation at known rates.
- **Comparative institutional design of accountability regimes is under-studied.** Evidence: Black et al. attribute fair-lending practice to supervisory examinations while LL 144 relies on transparency and employer discretion; Lacmanovic and Skare compare LL 144 and EU conformity assessments only from public reports; Burnat and Davidson model proxy compliance but do not test it. What would disprove it: cross-regime empirical comparisons of firm behavior under supervision-, transparency- and certification-based designs.

## Limitations of this scan

- Access depth: 3 of 16 main rows were read in full (Raji et al. 2020, Lam et al. 2024, Wright et al. 2024); 12 at abstract depth; 1 (Wagner et al. 2026) at metadata/snippet depth. All 31 supplementary items are abstract or metadata depth. Method details marked "inferred" were not read.
- Search coverage: 25 WebSearch queries on one day; domain filters leaked arXiv results for every publisher-restricted query, and the Wiley and INFORMS filters returned essentially no on-domain articles, so MIS Quarterly, Information Systems Research, Management Science, JAIS, Government Information Quarterly, Computer Law & Security Review, AI & Society, Business Horizons and Technological Forecasting and Social Change were not effectively searched; the only CLSR-adjacent hit (ScienceDirect pii S095058492600056X) turned out to be Information and Software Technology. The AIS eLibrary query surfaced BISE discussions and ECIS papers but no HICSS or ICIS paper on this topic.
- API coverage: Semantic Scholar answered HTTP 429 repeatedly and required back-off; its references/citations endpoints return at most the records requested (60 here) without totals, so citation chasing for the three central papers is partial and biased toward highly cited and 2026 items. OpenAlex answered 429 on both direct attempts; the verify_refs.py run may also show OpenAlex gaps. Crossref returned no abstract for most ACM proceedings papers, so their abstracts came from arXiv.
- Sites that refused fetches or were not attempted: ScienceDirect, ACM Digital Library, Wiley, SAGE, INFORMS, SSRN, nyc.gov (403), perma.cc (403). Springer article pages were not fetched (Crossref used instead). The HTTP arXiv API endpoint returned 403 once; HTTPS worked.
- Status labels depend on records seen on 2026-10-03; several 2026 FAccT and AIES items are accepted preprints whose proceedings records may differ, and the published versions of Mäntymäki et al., Batool et al. and Hadley et al. were not seen.
- Grey literature (Stanford AI Index 2026, PwC, McKinsey, HBR) was deliberately excluded from the evidence table, so practitioner survey statistics are not represented here.
- Date of all searches, fetches and API calls: 2026-10-03.
