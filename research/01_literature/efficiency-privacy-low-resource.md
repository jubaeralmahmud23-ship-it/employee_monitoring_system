## Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Kept: (a) peer-reviewed papers or clearly labelled preprints that make an empirical or systematic-review contribution to at least one of: AI adoption by SMEs or public bodies in developing economies, the generative-AI divide across countries or languages, on-premise or local LLM deployment feasibility and cost, quantization or compression effects across languages, or LLM capability in Bangla (Swahili and Indonesian were targeted but produced no tabulated paper); (b) one research-agenda paper in a target IS journal (Information Systems Frontiers) because it frames the IS research opportunity; (c) one public-sector benchmark (German) because it is the closest instance of a locally grounded organizational benchmark and exposes the absence of such benchmarks for the target languages.

Dropped: vendor blogs, glossaries, consultancy playbooks and forum snapshots (queries 2, 4, 19, 20); grey literature from UNDP, World Bank, ILO and OECD-Cisco (query 3) because the task asks for peer-reviewed or preprint evidence, although their numbers are cited as context in the synthesis; cryptographic private-inference preprints (query 6) because they target per-query confidentiality mechanisms rather than organizational deployment; position papers without data (arXiv:2506.02153); papers seen only as PDFs without recoverable venue metadata (openreview hits in query 14).

## Synthesis

### Agreements

Across the IS and CS strands, three points recur. First, language is treated as an independent barrier to AI value, not a proxy for income: Misra et al. estimate a roughly 20% adoption shortfall for low-resource-language countries after controlling for GDP, electricity, internet and age; Daepp and Slaughter see English substituting for poorly served local languages; Kabir et al. and Mahfuz et al. document poor Bangla generation and inefficient tokenization; Miranda et al. frame the same point as a "last mile" where multilinguality and edge deployment compete. Second, efficiency techniques are not language-neutral in the main empirical studies: Marchisio et al. and Marie and Fujita both report larger degradation for non-Latin-script and low-resource languages and for smaller models, and Chimoto et al. show calibration language is a lever. Third, on-premise deployment is technically feasible for light workloads but is justified in the literature mainly by privacy and predictable cost rather than by measured organizational value: Khalil et al. show near-cloud single-user quality on one consumer GPU, Pan et al. offer a break-even framework, and the SME adoption reviews (Kasigit et al., Mohammed et al.) list finance, skills and infrastructure as the binding constraints.

### Contradictions or tensions

(1) Borgersen and Goodwin report non-significant effects of calibration language under GGUF k-quantization for a 70B model on English and Norwegian, while Marchisio et al. (GPTQ, bitsandbytes; 8B-103B; non-Latin scripts; human evaluation) and Marie and Fujita (55 languages; 1.7B-70B; 2-bit to 4-bit) report disparate harm. The studies differ in quantizer family, model size, script and metric, and Marie and Fujita's finding that GGUF is the most robust format partially reconciles them, but no study holds these factors constant. (2) Khalil et al.'s framing ("comparable to cloud-based services") sits uneasily with their own result that per-request efficiency falls 75% from one to two concurrent users; the text I read contains no cost figures although the title and abstract promise SMB affordability. (3) Misra et al. conclude that "the only solution" is more training data, while TigerLLM claims a 1B-class Bangla model already beats GPT-3.5 and Mahfuz et al. argue that dedicated Bengali LLMs are needed but the data to build them do not exist; the evidence does not yet say whether model-side or data-side investment moves adoption. (4) The IS adoption studies (Kasigit et al., Mohammed et al.) explain non-adoption without language or compute variables, while the CS studies explain capability gaps without organizational variables; the reference lists of the three central papers show no cross-citation between the two strands. (5) Marchisio et al. show automatic metrics understate quantization harm by an order of magnitude in human evaluation, yet the efficiency and deployment papers in this set (Khalil, Pan, and the screened Cao et al. and Goldenits et al.) rely on automatic benchmarks alone.

### Methodological weaknesses

Country-level studies rest on single-vendor telemetry (Microsoft in Misra et al.; an unnamed free chatbot in Daepp and Slaughter) and on coarse language-to-country mappings that classify Indonesia as low-resource and Kenya as high-resource. Deployment studies are single-model, single-hardware, synthetic-load and localhost (Khalil et al.) or framework-level without published measurements in the abstract (Pan et al.). Quantization studies evaluate only languages the models already support (stated by Marchisio et al.), use machine translation or perplexity as the main outcome (Marie and Fujita; Chimoto et al.), and the null result (Borgersen and Goodwin) uses one large model and one benchmark. The IS evidence is a 32-article SLR and a 144-firm single-country survey. Seven of the sixteen rows are preprints, including the SMB server benchmark and both country-level diffusion studies; the cost-benefit framework (Pan et al.) is published at IEEE BigData 2025 but its numbers were not visible at abstract depth.

### Unresolved questions

Whether quantized 1-4B models retain usable quality on Bangla, Swahili or Indonesian business tasks (classification, extraction, summarization of customer or administrative text) when run on CPUs; what the cost-quality-privacy frontier looks like without a GPU and at LMIC hardware and electricity prices; whether local-language model quality, as opposed to finance and skills, changes SME or public-body adoption decisions; whether GGUF k-quantization is in fact language-neutral for non-Latin low-resource languages at small sizes; and whether locally grounded organizational benchmarks of the MÖVE type can be built for underserved languages at low cost.

## Candidate research gaps

- Gap 1, joint efficiency-by-language evaluation on organizational tasks: Marchisio et al. and Marie and Fujita show disparate quantization harm for non-Latin and low-resource languages, but on MMLU-style and MT benchmarks with languages the models support; BenLLM-Eval and Mahfuz et al. show weak open-model Bangla performance at full precision; no study in this scan evaluates quantized small models (1-4B) on Bangla, Swahili or Indonesian business-text tasks under CPU constraints. Disproved if a paper in Miranda et al.'s 232-paper survey or in the LowResNLP 2025 workshop already reports such an evaluation with per-language, per-bit-width results on organizational tasks.
- Gap 2, costed CPU-only deployment frontier for SMEs and public bodies in developing economies: Khalil et al. use an RTX 5090, Pan et al. (IEEE BigData 2025) assume GPU servers and subscription pricing, and neither reports LMIC price levels; practitioner blogs claim 10-25 tokens/s on CPU but no peer-reviewed measurement of quality-per-dollar or quality-per-watt for CPU-only small models on organizational workloads was found. Disproved if a peer-reviewed TCO study with CPU-only measurements for small organizations exists (search terms used here may have missed systems venues such as MLSys or IEEE edge-computing conferences, which were not domain-filtered).
- Gap 3, bridging firm-level adoption models and technical language/compute barriers: Misra et al. show a country-level language effect on adoption; Kasigit et al. and Mohammed et al. model SME adoption without language or deployment-mode variables; no firm-level study measures whether local-language model performance or on-premise availability shifts adoption intention or use. Disproved if an IS study (e.g., in EJISDC, ITD or Information Systems Frontiers, whose venue-filtered searches here returned nothing relevant) includes language fit or local deployability as a construct.
- Gap 4, unresolved contradiction on whether quantization is language-neutral: Borgersen and Goodwin (null, GGUF, 70B, Norwegian) vs Marchisio et al. and Marie and Fujita (harm, GPTQ/AWQ/bitsandbytes and multiple formats, smaller models, non-Latin scripts); Chimoto et al. show calibration language matters for GPTQ/AWQ at 7-8B. A controlled replication crossing quantizer family (GGUF k-quant vs GPTQ/AWQ), model size (1-8B) and script (Bangla, Swahili, Indonesian) with the same tasks has not been found. Disproved if such a factorial replication exists; it is feasible on this project's CPU-only hardware for 1-4B models.
- Gap 5, locally grounded organizational benchmarks for underserved languages: MÖVE (German) and the Dutch and other German public-sector benchmarks seen in query 19 show the value of task- and value-specific local evaluation, and MÖVE finds model size is a poor predictor; no comparable public-sector or SME benchmark for Bangla, Swahili or Indonesian surfaced (SOMAJGYAAN covers Bangla culture and social knowledge, not administrative tasks). Disproved if a public-administration or SME-task benchmark in one of these languages exists on the ACL Anthology or Hugging Face.
