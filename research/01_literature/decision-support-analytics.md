## Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Kept:
- Papers that appeared in a search result or API response received in this run and whose abstract (at least) could be read, on: text-to-SQL and NL interfaces to business data, LLMs over tabular or time-series data, LLM forecasting claims and their critiques, and experiments on human reliance on AI or LLM advice.
- Benchmarks, surveys, negative or critical results, and human-subject experiments, because each informs a different kind of gap. Peer-reviewed venues were preferred (NeurIPS, ICLR, PVLDB, TKDE, TMLR, IUI, CHI, Scientific Reports, International Journal of Forecasting, ACM TiiS). Two central items are still preprints or were updated after publication (BEAVER v3; the overreliance position paper) and are labelled as such.
- A press item (HBR digital article) is listed only in the supplementary list, labelled "other" and metadata/snippet, because the experiment behind it did not surface in a peer-reviewed form.

Dropped:
- Vendor and consultancy blogs, Medium posts, Substack posts, mirror sites (alphaxiv, athina.ai, papers.cool, catalyzex, sotaverified, gitee, sourcepulse), and news coverage; none is used as evidence.
- Clinical decision-support papers returned by the ScienceDirect and PMC results (tumour boards, emergency medicine, plastic surgery): outside the business-analytics scope.
- Method papers that only propose a new text-to-SQL pipeline without a benchmark, evaluation or human finding (many of the arXiv items in queries 4, 13, 15, 16), because the gap analysis needs evidence about validity and use, not another pipeline.
- Items whose title or abstract could not be seen (for example the ICIS 2024 PDF in query 8, whose title was not shown).

## Synthesis

### Agreements

- Across the three benchmark papers read in full and the BEAVER and BIRD abstracts, enterprise-style text-to-SQL is far from solved: the best reported numbers fall from above 90% on Spider 1.0 and 73% on BIRD to 21.3% (Spider 2.0 agentic), 5.7-23% (Spider 2.0-lite) and 10.8% (BEAVER with GPT-5.2). The failure modes agree: schema linking on very wide or nested schemas, dialect functions, multi-step query planning, and grounding external documentation.
- Benchmark validity is now an explicit research object. Jin et al. show that more than half of the audited BIRD Mini-Dev and Spider 2.0-snow examples contain annotation errors and that correcting 100 BIRD examples reorders the leaderboard (rank correlation 0.32). Spider 2.0's own authors describe iterative correction rounds; the TKDE survey and the PVLDB tutorial both make evaluation and error analysis first-class lifecycle stages; SQUAB and the calibration paper treat ambiguity and confidence as the next problems.
- On human reliance, Cecil et al. (classification advice), Spatharioti et al. (LLM search) and the position paper by Ibrahim et al. agree that people fail to reject incorrect AI output and that this is a design and measurement problem, not only a model-accuracy problem. Spatharioti et al. and the Behaviour & Information Technology snippet suggest that targeted, non-verbal signals (highlighting, numerical cues) help more than verbal explanations.
- On forecasting, Tan et al., the ACL 2025 noise paper, the time-series benchmarking critiques (queries 14 and 17) and Dai et al. converge on scepticism about pretrained language backbones as general forecasters; Abolghasemi et al. add that 2023-era LLMs did not consistently beat 123 human retail forecasters.

### Contradictions or tensions

- Gruver et al. (NeurIPS 2023) report zero-shot LLMs matching or exceeding purpose-built forecasters; Tan et al. (NeurIPS 2024) find the LLM component adds nothing in three adapter-based methods. The regimes differ (frozen zero-shot prompting vs fine-tuned backbones; different datasets), so the disagreement is about what "LLMs for forecasting" means, and neither paper evaluates business series with promotions or calendar effects.
- Schoenegger et al. find LLM assistants raise human forecasting accuracy by 24-28% (even a deliberately noisy assistant helps), whereas the HBR executive study (press-level evidence only) reports that ChatGPT made managers more optimistic and less accurate, and Abolghasemi et al. find LLMs alone not consistently better than humans. Task type (geopolitical binary questions vs stock-price point forecasts vs retail demand) and the comparison condition (weaker LLM vs peer discussion vs no aid) differ, so the direction of the LLM-advice effect is unresolved.
- Explanations: Schemmer et al. report that explanations shift appropriateness of reliance; Cecil et al. find heatmaps and charts do not reduce overreliance; Spatharioti et al. find colour-coded highlighting does help. The common thread is that the format and the content (where the error is) matter more than "explanation" as such.
- Benchmark numbers vs benchmark validity: BIRD's 92.96% human reference and all leaderboard scores were computed against gold queries that Jin et al. find erroneous in about half of the audited cases; Spider 2.0's "not easier" claim for the lite setting and BEAVER's changing dataset sizes across versions mean headline accuracies are not stable quantities.

### Methodological weaknesses

- Closed-model dependence: the headline results (o1-preview, GPT-5.2, o3 for SAR-Agent, LLaMA-3 as judge in InsightBench) are not reproducible without paid APIs; open models evaluated on Spider 2.0 are 72B-405B parameters. This matters for a zero-budget CPU-only project.
- All-or-nothing execution accuracy on single queries dominates; BEAVER's subtask annotations and Jin et al.'s error patterns are the only fine-grained diagnostics seen; InsightBench substitutes an LLM judge for human validation of "insights".
- Benchmark construction: Spider 2.0 uses public cloud datasets as a stand-in for enterprise data; BEAVER's private warehouse data cannot be fully released; both rely on small author teams for annotation, and Jin et al. show what that costs.
- Human-subject studies use students or online panels, single sessions, and advice formats (classification labels, search answers, binary forecasts) that are not analytic outputs such as SQL results, charts or KPI tables. None of the reliance studies seen here presents participants with LLM-generated query results.
- Forecasting evaluations reuse the same eight to thirteen datasets; the benchmarking critiques in query 14 note contamination (one snippet: only 6% of datasets never used in pre-training) and the accuracy-calibration trade-off.

### Unresolved questions

1. How much of the reported progress on text-to-SQL survives annotation correction and ambiguity-aware evaluation, and does any benchmark measure whether a BI user would reach the right decision?
2. Do decision-makers detect wrong but plausible SQL-generated numbers, and which signals (confidence, abstention, highlighting of the uncertain clause) change that?
3. Under what task structure does LLM forecasting advice help rather than inflate confidence (binary event forecasts vs operational demand with promotions)?
4. Can small open models (1-4B) with retrieval or constrained decoding reach usable accuracy on enterprise-style schemas, and at what compute cost relative to the value added?
5. What is the decision-quality counterpart of execution accuracy, and can it be measured without an LLM judge?

## Candidate research gaps

- **Gap 1: Benchmark validity to deployment validity.** Evidence: enterprise accuracies of 5.7-23% (Spider 2.0), 10.8% (BEAVER) and annotation error rates above 50% (Jin et al.) coexist with vendor-style claims of production readiness (NL2SQL360). No paper seen links benchmark error to downstream decision error. Would be disproved by: a published study that maps NL2SQL errors onto business decisions (for example wrong KPI values accepted by analysts), or by corrected benchmarks on which rankings are stable.
- **Gap 2: Overreliance on analytic (not classificatory) LLM output.** Evidence: reliance experiments use labels, search answers or diagrams (Cecil, Spatharioti, Anthis); none presents SQL-derived tables or charts with injected errors. Would be disproved by: finding experiments where participants audit LLM-generated query results or dashboards with known errors (search of CHI 2026, IUI and ISR proceedings needed).
- **Gap 3: Direction of the LLM-advice effect in business forecasting.** Evidence: positive (Schoenegger), null-to-negative (Abolghasemi; HBR press item) results on different task types; Tan et al. undermine the model-side claim. Would be disproved by: an IJF or EJOR experiment in which forecasters receive LLM advice on retail or demand series with promotions, with a no-aid and a statistical-aid control.
- **Gap 4: Ambiguity, calibration and abstention in NL-to-data interfaces as a human factor.** Evidence: SQUAB and the EMNLP calibration paper produce model-side measures; Lee (2026, preprint) scores abstention and clarification as correct responses and Wu (2026, preprint) proposes structural abstention with a user confirmation step but reports no metrics; Spatharioti et al. show a highlighting cue helps. No paper seen tests whether calibrated confidence, clarification questions or abstention change what users decide. Would be disproved by: a user study that manipulates NL2SQL confidence display, clarification or abstention and measures reliance and decision accuracy.
- **Gap 5: A validated measure of decision quality for analytics agents.** Evidence: InsightBench uses planted insights and an LLM judge; AoR (Schemmer) exists for advice-taking but not for multi-step analytics; the position paper by Ibrahim et al. lists measurement gaps. Two 2026 preprints found by forward chasing move toward outcome-based scoring (Lee's "Business Truth Rate"; Argo-Bench grades actions by simulator consequences), but both are unreviewed, use synthetic warehouses, and score agents rather than human decisions made with agent output. Would be disproved by: a validated instrument or benchmark that scores analytics output by the decision a person makes with it, or peer-reviewed versions of the two preprints with human validation.
- **Gap 6: Small open models under enterprise schema scale and the cost-value trade-off.** Evidence: Spider 2.0 evaluates 72B-405B open models only; Tan et al. show LLM components cost up to three orders of magnitude more compute for no forecasting gain; Lee (2026, preprint) reports a rule-gated 7B agent beating a 32B baseline on a synthetic warehouse benchmark, which narrows but does not close the gap (7B, synthetic data, single author, no ablation). Nothing seen evaluates 1-4B quantised models on Spider 2.0-lite, BIRD Mini-Dev (corrected) or BEAVER, nor reports cost per correct answer on those public sets. Would be disproved by: a paper or leaderboard entry reporting such models on these sets. This gap is the one most directly testable from this session's CPU-only environment, with the caveat that Spider 2.0's cloud databases need BigQuery or Snowflake accounts, so Spider 2.0-lite's SQLite/DuckDB subset, BIRD Mini-Dev and the corrected BIRD subset are the feasible targets.

## Limitations of this scan

- **Access depth per row.** 3 of 16 main-table rows were read in full text (arXiv HTML of Tan et al., Lei et al., Jin et al.); 13 at abstract depth (arXiv API, Crossref or Semantic Scholar abstracts); 0 at snippet depth. Of the 15 supplementary items, 7 are abstract depth and 8 metadata/snippet. Findings in abstract-depth rows are limited to what abstracts state; sample sizes and task details that abstracts omit are marked "not shown" or inferred and labelled as such. Numbers quoted from the three full-text papers come from their main text and tables as rendered in arXiv HTML; appendix tables were not checked.
- **Search engine and API coverage.** 21 WebSearch queries (3 extended) is a landscape sweep, not exhaustive coverage. Domain filters for pubsonline.informs.org, sciencedirect.com and aisel.aisnet.org returned mostly leaked arXiv or PMC items; no Management Science, ISR, Decision Support Systems, EJOR or MISQ paper on this topic surfaced, which should be read as a limitation of the search route (those publishers refuse automated fetches and their pages rank poorly in the engine) rather than as evidence that such papers do not exist. KDD, SIGMOD and ACL/EMNLP were reached only through dl.acm.org and aclanthology.org filters and through Semantic Scholar venues.
- **Citation chasing.** Backward lists were truncated to the first 60 references and forward lists to the first 100 citations as returned by Semantic Scholar, then sorted by citation count; for 2024-2026 papers, forward citation counts are small and the ordering is noisy. The second and third chases needed 20-40 second pauses after HTTP 429 responses. OpenAlex could not be used as a fallback during this run (429 on every call).
- **Sites that refused fetches.** dl.acm.org, sciencedirect.com, ieeexplore.ieee.org, onlinelibrary.wiley.com, pubsonline.informs.org, tandfonline.com and hbr.org were not fetched (bot protection, per the environment facts); their items are recorded from search snippets plus Crossref or Semantic Scholar metadata. GitHub repository pages answered 403 to curl HEAD from this session and were not opened; GitHub hosting claims rest on paper text and search results. The ACL Anthology page for SQUAB rendered its abstract inside an editing form, which was parsed by hand.
- **Verification.** OpenAlex columns are empty (429) for all 20 titles, so open-access status and cited-by counts from OpenAlex are missing; Crossref and arXiv columns were complete.
- **Date.** All searches, fetches and API calls were made on 2026-10-03. Versions matter in this area: BEAVER's abstract changed between arXiv versions, Spider 2.0's gold queries were updated on 2025-10-29 after the Jin et al. audit, and the TKDE survey is at arXiv v7.
