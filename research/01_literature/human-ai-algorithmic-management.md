## Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Kept:
- Empirical, review or theory papers on algorithmic management, algorithmic control, electronic performance monitoring, AI-based workplace surveillance, worker trust, fairness, resistance, regulation, human oversight, human-AI complementarity and advice-taking, where the record appeared in a search result or API response received in this run and at least its abstract could be read.
- Peer-reviewed journal and conference papers first; one working paper was kept in the main table because it is central to a gap (Kala and Lyons NBER w33348) and is labelled as such. Dong et al. was recorded from its arXiv preprint but the verification run showed a peer-reviewed AIES 2025 version, so it is labelled peer-reviewed. Research-in-progress conference papers are labelled "preliminary" where the abstract says so.
- Both critical (labor process, CSCW, business ethics) and managerial (IS, OB, economics) perspectives, so that the synthesis can show tensions rather than one framing.

Dropped:
- Vendor, law-firm, consultancy and "statistics" pages (queries 4, 14, 24): their adoption percentages and regulatory timelines are unverified and are not cited as evidence.
- Items whose abstract could not be read and whose title alone did not clearly address the area (for example the Journal of Business and Psychology 2026 item in query 2 and the JBR 2025 item in query 13 were left unopened for time reasons and are only mentioned in the log).
- Papers about AI and employment in general (job loss estimates, arXiv 2509.15265) and about surveillance outside work (carceral surveillance beyond the workshop paper).
- Systems-engineering papers that build monitoring pipelines without studying workers (the Procedia CS 2025 computer-vision employee monitoring system is noted only as evidence that such systems are being built).
- Mirrors on ResearchGate and news coverage when the primary record was available.

## Synthesis

### Agreements

- Across methods and decades, monitoring does not raise measured performance on average. The Ravid et al. meta-analysis (K = 94) finds a corrected correlation of -.01 between EPM presence and performance, the Kala and Lyons RCT finds no average effect of digital surveillance on output, and the CHB Reports 2022 meta-analysis cited in query 15 reports r = -0.01 (snippet only). What monitoring reliably moves is stress and privacy-invasion perceptions (Ravid et al.; Glavin et al.; Hennum Nilsson et al.; Sum et al.).
- The framing of monitoring to workers matters more than its mere presence: justification (Kala and Lyons), stated purpose and transparency (Ravid et al. moderators), worker choice within AI recommendations (Chan et al.) and interpretable, correctable rules (Arunkumar et al.) each change outcomes in the direction of better attitudes or output.
- Algorithmic control is described as more comprehensive, instantaneous, interactive and opaque than earlier rational control (Kellogg et al.) and as mostly used in a controlling rather than enabling way (Noponen et al.), with gig and warehouse settings as the main evidence base (Lee et al.; Möhlmann et al.; Cram et al.; Cheon and Erickson).
- Worker resistance is a stable finding from 2015 forums (Lee et al.) through algoactivism (Kellogg et al.) to Reddit commiseration, obfuscation and soldiering (Sum et al.) and warehouse work games (Cheon and Erickson). These behaviors are also measurement threats for any productivity-scoring system.
- Supervisors and managers are themselves subjects of the systems (Sum et al.; Oezdemir et al.), and employers in the OECD survey cite unclear accountability and inability to follow the tools' logic as their own concerns.

### Contradictions or tensions

- Transparency as remedy. Ravid et al. find more transparent monitoring is associated with better attitudes; Cram et al. find that perceived algorithmic-control transparency does not mitigate threat technostress; Arunkumar et al. report a non-monotonic pattern where partial interpretability is worst; Sum et al. report that some managers withhold details to reduce anxiety while workers say opacity worsens it. The constructs differ (purpose justification, mechanism explanation, interpretability), and no study found manipulates them side by side.
- Human oversight. Schauer et al. (preliminary lab result) find oversight improves decision performance and trust; Oezdemir et al. find supervisors' oversight becomes nominal; Vaccaro et al. find human-AI combinations underperform the better party in decision tasks, with the human making the final call in more than 95% of designs. Regulation that mandates oversight (the EU AI Act, as motivated in Schauer et al. and Heimbach et al.) is therefore resting on contested evidence.
- Algorithm appreciation versus aversion. Logg et al. show lay people prefer algorithmic advice, but Moritz and Schmidt and Klöpper and Messer show that being managed or rewarded by an algorithm lowers justice and trust and raises retaliation; Dong et al. show a third pattern in which AI evaluation is accepted even when it is harsher. The role of the person (advisee versus subject) appears to flip the sign.
- Field versus laboratory. The two field experiments (Chan et al.; Kala and Lyons) find effects that depend on configuration and framing; the laboratory and vignette studies in Ravid's corpus produce the most negative attitude effects (vignette rho = -.32 versus non-experiment rho = .00 for broad attitudes), suggesting that hypothetical monitoring is judged more harshly than experienced monitoring.
- Platform versus employment. The IS-journal anchors (Möhlmann et al.; Cram et al.; Wiener et al.) study Uber, whereas the recent CSCW and economics studies (Sum et al.; Kala and Lyons) study remote employees and online labor; whether gig findings transfer to employer-employee monitoring is asserted (Jarrahi et al.; Zhang et al.) more than tested.

### Methodological weaknesses

- Most quantitative worker-side evidence is cross-sectional self-report (Cram et al.; Glavin et al.; Hennum Nilsson et al.; Röttgen et al.; Arunkumar et al.), with monitoring intensity measured as perception rather than from system logs.
- Meta-analytic cells are thin: Ravid et al. note K < 5 for many typology cells and could not test interactions among covarying characteristics; Vaccaro et al. had only 3 experiments with pre-determined task delegation and I-squared near 98%.
- Several of the newest findings are research-in-progress conference papers (Schauer et al.; Amoah and Mehta; Moritz and Schmidt) or preprints (Arunkumar et al.; Dong et al. was read only in its preprint form), and the strongest field experiment (Chan et al.) is a conference version without a located journal version.
- Qualitative samples are small or non-representative by the authors' own account (Sum et al.: Reddit demographics, 10 interviews; Oezdemir et al.: 12 interviews).
- The EPM meta-analytic corpus ends in February 2021 and the human-AI corpus in June 2023, so AI-based productivity scoring, emotion recognition and LLM-based evaluation are essentially absent from pooled evidence.
- Citation-chasing in this scan was partial: the Semantic Scholar references endpoint returned nothing for the two older central papers, and forward citations sorted by count favor reviews.

### Unresolved questions

- Does AI-based evaluation (as opposed to rule-based EPM) change the psychological contract, as Dong et al. suggest, and does the muted emotional response hold in real employment over time?
- Which form of transparency (justification of purpose, explanation of mechanism, contestability) changes worker outcomes, and for whom?
- Under what organizational conditions does a human overseer of a monitoring system have substantive rather than nominal authority, and does oversight improve worker outcomes or only decision accuracy?
- How large is the gap between what employers say monitoring is for (the OECD survey) and what the system logs show it is used for, and can that gap be measured without participants?
- How do regulatory requirements (EU AI Act high-risk obligations, GDPR DPIAs, US scheduling laws) get encoded into monitoring software, and where does encoding fail (Lynn et al.)? The 2026 timeline of AI Act obligations was only visible in unverified vendor pages in this scan.

## Candidate research gaps

- **Gap 1: AI-specific monitoring is missing from pooled evidence.** Evidence: the Ravid et al. corpus ends February 2021 and does not include AI productivity scoring or emotion AI; Vaccaro et al. cover decision and creation tasks, not management; the only AI-manager experiment found in this scan (Dong et al., AIES 2025) is a single study in a simulated Minecraft workplace. What would disprove it: a 2024-2026 meta-analysis or multi-site field study of AI-based EPM showing effects indistinguishable from classic EPM, which this scan did not find (the JBP 2026 item in query 2 was not opened and should be checked).
- **Gap 2: Human oversight of monitoring systems is mandated but under-tested.** Evidence: one preliminary lab experiment (Schauer et al.) supports oversight, one interview study (Oezdemir et al.) finds it nominal, the largest meta-analysis (Vaccaro et al.) finds human-final-decision designs lose to the better party in decision tasks, and the only regulation-in-practice study found in this scan (Lynn et al.) covers US scheduling only. What would disprove it: field experiments or audits showing that oversight configurations change fairness or worker outcomes robustly, or published evaluations of AI Act oversight in employment settings.
- **Gap 3: Monitoring exposure is measured by perception, not by system telemetry.** Evidence: the quantitative studies in the table use self-report scales (COMAMA is the only validated one seen in this scan); none links logged monitoring intensity to outcomes; Kala and Lyons have objective output but a short online task. What would disprove it: a body of studies pairing monitoring-system logs with worker outcomes (none surfaced in 24 queries; publisher-walled venues may hold some).
- **Gap 4: Transparency is treated as one thing.** Evidence: Ravid et al. (positive), Cram et al. (no mitigation), Arunkumar et al. (non-monotonic) and Kala and Lyons (justification matters) use different transparency constructs and reach different conclusions; no study found manipulates purpose justification, mechanism explanation and contestability together. What would disprove it: an experiment or a measurement paper that separates these constructs and shows they behave alike.
- **Gap 5: Evidence is concentrated in gig and warehouse work while adoption is now in standard firms.** Evidence: the IS anchors study Uber; the OECD employer survey reports common AM use across six countries' firms; the remote-employee evidence is one Reddit-based CSCW paper and one NBER working paper. What would disprove it: a set of peer-reviewed studies of AI monitoring in standard employment with comparable designs (Reimann 2025 and Dupuis 2025 in NTWE, seen only as titles, may be partial counter-evidence and should be read).

## Limitations of this scan

- Access depth: of the 16 main rows, 4 were read in full (Kellogg et al. from a public-access PDF; Ravid et al. from a third-party mirror; Vaccaro et al. and Sum et al. from arXiv HTML) and 12 at abstract level; of the 28 supplementary rows, 18 are abstract-level and 10 are metadata or snippet only. Findings columns for abstract-level rows contain only what the abstract states; for Chan et al. and Kala and Lyons, numbers that appear only in news or summary snippets are marked as such.
- Search coverage: 24 WebSearch queries (4 extended), run on one day, English only. Domain-filtered queries to pubsonline.informs.org returned nothing from INFORMS, so MISQ, ISR and Organization Science coverage relied on open queries and API records; dl.acm.org, sciencedirect.com, onlinelibrary.wiley.com and link.springer.com surfaced records but their pages were not fetched (bot protection), so their content is known only from snippets and Crossref or Semantic Scholar metadata. No query was run on eur-lex.europa.eu, so statements about the EU AI Act timeline are not verified here.
- API coverage: Semantic Scholar returned no reference lists for the two older central papers in two attempts; its citation lists are complete for Lee et al. (914) but truncated at 1,000 of 2,205 for Kellogg et al.; OpenAlex was used for only three records to respect rate limits; Crossref returned no abstract for 8 of 16 DOI lookups.
- Citation chasing favored highly cited reviews over primary studies, and the forward-citation lists were not screened beyond their top entries.
- Sites that refused fetches in this run: link.springer.com and bmcpsychology.biomedcentral.com (Client Challenge), eurofound.europa.eu (HTTP 429), api.osf.io (401 without credentials; the view-only page itself loaded). Not attempted because of known bot protection: dl.acm.org, sciencedirect.com, onlinelibrary.wiley.com, pubsonline.informs.org, misq.umn.edu.
- Datasets: no microdata on AI-based employee monitoring was found; the European surveys exist but their access routes were not verified from this session, and the only Hugging Face hits are uninspected small datasets of unknown provenance.
- Date: all statements reflect what was visible on 2026-10-03; several 2025-2026 items are conference versions or preprints whose published versions may since have appeared.
