## Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Kept:
- Empirical or conceptual work on how organizations and their employees adopt, use, govern or experience generative AI / LLMs, where the result appeared in a search I actually ran.
- Field experiments, RCTs, large surveys, register-linked studies, qualitative case studies, and IS editorials/agenda pieces, because each informs a different kind of gap.
- Working papers (NBER, SSRN, arXiv) when they are central to the productivity debate; they are labelled "preprint".
- One research-organization report (METR) because it is the most-cited negative result on developer productivity; labelled "other".

Dropped:
- Vendor blogs, consultancy surveys and news-site summaries (Informatica, AvePoint, Worklytics, G2, Fortune, eWeek, Medium, Substack, etc.). They were used only to resolve titles or figures of underlying studies, never as evidence rows.
- Patents, healthcare-specific GenAI roadmaps, consumer-health shadow AI, metaverse business models, and education-only studies, because they are outside the organizational IS scope.
- Items whose relevance could not be judged from the snippet (e.g., ACL tutorials index, PMC papers on unrelated workplace interventions).
- Studies with no visible venue and no visible findings were kept out of the evidence table unless the title alone signals a central theme (then marked "finding not visible").

Status labels follow what was visible: "peer-reviewed" only when a journal or conference proceedings name was shown; "preprint" for arXiv/NBER/SSRN; "other" for research-organization or government reports; "unknown" otherwise.

## Synthesis

### Agreements
- Across the randomized and quasi-experimental studies visible here (customer support, consulting, professional writing, software development, integrated office tools), access to GenAI raises task-level speed and often graded quality, with typical headline effects of roughly 12-40% on the measured task.
- The gains are consistently heterogeneous: less experienced or lower-performing workers gain most (QJE customer support, BCG consultants, Management Science developers, Noy & Zhang), which several studies read as skill-leveling.
- Adoption is individually driven and often informal: US survey data show rapid personal uptake, ICIS 2025 field work finds a "personal-first orientation" rather than top-down formalization, and shadow-AI theory argues that ad hoc use becomes normalized. Governance frameworks lag use in nearly every source that discusses governance.
- Worker experience is two-sided. Both the quantitative survey literature (alienation and expediency; technostress; GenAI literacy as a moderator) and the qualitative organizational studies (national lab concerns about data, publishing and jobs) report costs alongside benefits.
- IS-venue work is largely qualitative, single-site, or conceptual (case studies, interviews, editorials, design-research), while the causal evidence sits in economics and management journals or working papers.

### Contradictions or tensions
- Task-level gains versus organization-level nulls. Danish register-linked estimates bound earnings and hours effects at under 2% two years after adoption, executives in the 2026 NBER survey mostly report no discernible productivity or employment effect, and the UK government Copilot trial reports time savings without evidence of productivity improvement. The experimental literature and the outcome literature therefore disagree, or at least measure different things.
- Skill-leveling versus expert harm. The same QJE study that shows leveling also shows small quality declines for the most experienced agents; the Alibaba field experiment shows top performers' quality falling; METR finds experienced OSS developers 19% slower. The leveling narrative may hide a redistribution rather than a Pareto improvement.
- Perceived versus measured productivity. METR participants believed they were 20% faster while being 19% slower. Most IS surveys in this area rely on perceived usefulness and self-reported outcomes, so the direction of bias is a live issue for the survey-based IS literature.
- Inconsistent reporting within sources: the Microsoft Copilot study appears with 56 firms/6,000+ workers in one snippet and 66 firms/7,137 workers in another (likely different versions); the NBER 34984 sample is ~750 executives on nber.org but ~6,000 in press summaries. Neither could be resolved without full text.
- Trust: willingness to delegate drops sharply with responsibility level, yet organizations are reported to be moving toward "workflow agent" modalities; trust requirements also differ by department within the same firm.

### Methodological weaknesses (as visible or inferred)
- Short horizons: most experiments run weeks to months; the only multi-year outcome evidence is register-based and shows nulls.
- Single-firm or single-tool designs dominate; vendor involvement (Microsoft, Alibaba) in several of the largest experiments raises independence questions, which the snippets do not address.
- Outcome measures are mostly activity counts, time, or graded quality on researcher-chosen tasks; organization-level value, error propagation, and verification labor are rarely measured.
- Survey-based IS studies are cross-sectional and self-reported; constructs such as "GenAI use" are often not tied to a specific artifact or configuration, the very problem the ISJ editorial raises.
- Qualitative organizational studies have small samples (20-66 participants) and single sites.
- Shadow-use and disclosure work is largely conceptual or relies on self-report, with no behavioral trace data visible.

### Unresolved questions
- Where do task-level time savings go? Reallocation to new tasks, verification work, idle time, or output quality, and under what organizational conditions do they aggregate to measurable value?
- Is the expert-worker decline in quality a transient learning effect, an interface problem, or structural?
- What determines whether employees disclose AI use, and how does concealment interact with monitoring, performance evaluation, and governance?
- Do guardrail-style governance mechanisms change behavior, and can that be shown causally rather than through maturity models?
- How should "GenAI use" be operationalized so that different studies are comparable?

## Candidate research gaps

- Micro-to-macro translation of productivity gains. Evidence: large task-level gains in RCTs coexist with precise null effects on hours and earnings (Denmark), executive reports of no impact (NBER 34984), and a government pilot showing time savings without productivity. What would disprove it: organization-level studies that link telemetry-measured GenAI use to business outcomes (throughput, error rates, cycle time) over a year or more and find consistent positive effects, or a visible explanation (e.g., reallocation to new tasks) that reconciles the two literatures.
- Expert-worker and verification-labor downside. Evidence: quality declines for top performers (QJE, Alibaba), slower experienced developers (METR), and governance work noting that verification is skipped under time pressure. What would disprove it: field studies with experienced workers showing no quality loss once interface or training conditions are met, or showing the effect vanishes with newer tools.
- Behavioral measurement of shadow and undisclosed use. Evidence: shadow-AI theory (DART), the "personal-first" ICIS field study, and disclosure hesitancy figures are all self-report or conceptual; no behavioral dataset of sanctioned vs. unsanctioned use was found. What would disprove it: discovery of an open log-level or trace dataset distinguishing sanctioned and unsanctioned tool use in a real organization, or published studies using such data.
- Causal evaluation of governance-of-use mechanisms. Evidence: only one quasi-experimental guardrails study surfaced; the rest are frameworks, maturity models and policy recommendations. What would disprove it: multiple field or quasi-experiments showing which policy, technical and workflow controls change employee behavior and risk outcomes.
- Artifact under-specification in IS GenAI studies. Evidence: the ISJ editorial states that submissions often treat GenAI as a black box, and many survey studies here measure "GenAI use" generically. What would disprove it: a body of IS studies that specify model, integration mode (chat vs. embedded copilot vs. agent) and configuration and show results differ by these.
- Trust and delegation heterogeneity within firms. Evidence: trust factors vary by department (EIS 2025) and delegation willingness falls with responsibility (longitudinal study), while deployments move toward agentic modalities. What would disprove it: studies showing stable, uniform trust and delegation patterns across units and task-responsibility levels.

## Limitations of this scan

- Snippet-only access: no paper was opened; findings, sample sizes and venues are whatever the WebSearch tool surfaced. Where two snippets disagreed (Copilot study size; NBER 34984 sample), both figures are recorded and neither is verified.
- Search engine coverage: domain filters for AISeL, INFORMS, OpenReview/NeurIPS/ACL frequently returned arXiv or PMC mirrors instead of the target host, so MISQ, ISR, JMIS, EJIS, JAIS and DSS content is under-represented; only ISJ, IJIM, BISE, EIS, TFSC and the CHB family surfaced with venue visible.
- No citation chasing: scholarly APIs (Crossref, OpenAlex, Semantic Scholar, DBLP) were blocked, so forward and backward citation tracing was impossible and the paper set reflects search ranking, not citation structure.
- Non-systematic: 30 ad hoc queries on one day (2026-10-03); no screening counts, no dual review, no protocol. This is a landscape sweep to locate gaps, not a review.
- Preprint share is high: several central items (Copilot experiment, Danish null results, executive survey, Alibaba experiment, landscape review) are working papers or arXiv preprints and may change on publication.
- Author names and years were frequently absent from snippets and are marked as such rather than inferred.
