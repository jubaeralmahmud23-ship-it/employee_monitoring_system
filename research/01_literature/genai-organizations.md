## Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Kept:
- Empirical or conceptual work on how organizations and their employees adopt, use, govern or experience generative AI / LLMs, where the record appeared in a search or API response received in this run and its abstract (at least) could be read, or where the title alone is central to the debate (two such rows are marked metadata/snippet).
- Field experiments, RCTs, representative surveys, register-linked studies, interpretive case studies, an editorial, a theory paper and a review of reviews, because each informs a different kind of gap.
- Working papers and preprints (NBER, SSRN, arXiv) when they are central to the productivity debate; they are labelled "preprint". Where Crossref shows a later peer-reviewed version, the row cites both and the status reflects the published version.
- One research-organization RCT (METR) because it is the most-discussed negative result on developer productivity; it exists as an arXiv preprint and is labelled accordingly.

Dropped:
- Vendor blogs, consultancy surveys, newsletters, mailing-list posts and news summaries (Substack, Medium, getcoai.com, Superblocks, Mend, Adaptive Security, Sprinto, eWeek, the-decoder, diginomica, Marginal Revolution, flowingdata, tagteam feeds). They were used only to locate underlying studies, never as evidence rows, and their headline figures (for example "95% of pilots fail", "89% report no change") are not reported as findings.
- Items outside the organizational IS scope: consumer-health shadow AI, metaverse business models, entrepreneurship in Kenya, education-only studies, healthcare roadmaps, HR-journal papers on recruitment, patents.
- Items whose relevance could not be judged beyond the title, and duplicate copies of the same paper on ResearchGate, ezproxy or personal sites.
- Semantic Scholar's abstract for Feuerriegel et al. (BISE 2024) was visibly mismatched (it describes GANs and VAEs); it was discarded and the item is listed as metadata only.

Status labels: "peer-reviewed" only when Crossref, the AIS eLibrary or the ACM/INFORMS/Wiley/Elsevier record shows a journal or proceedings; "preprint" for arXiv/NBER/SSRN/BFI; "other" for an editorial in a peer-reviewed journal (it is not itself a reviewed study); "unknown" otherwise.

## Synthesis

### Agreements
- Every causal study read here that measures a bounded task (customer support at one firm and at Alibaba, consulting tasks at BCG, professional writing, three developer RCTs, product innovation at P&G) finds positive average effects on speed or graded quality, typically in the 12-40% range on the measured task. Access to an integrated tool in ordinary office work (Dillon et al.) also shows savings, but only on tasks a worker controls alone (email), not on coordinated activity (meetings, document authorship).
- Heterogeneity by prior skill or tenure is the most replicated pattern: novices and lower performers gain most (Brynjolfsson et al., Dell'Acqua et al. 2023, Cui et al., Noy and Zhang, Alibaba), while the most experienced see no gain or a quality decline (Brynjolfsson et al.'s top quintile, Alibaba's top performers, METR's experienced developers).
- Adoption is individual-first and runs ahead of organizational formalization: nationally representative US surveys show rapid personal uptake (Bick et al.), Dillon et al. observe that firm identity explains usage better than industry or role, Cohen and Zalmanson describe a "personal-first orientation", Schneider et al. argue that the governance object is constituted through use, and the IS review of reviews calls the result a governance vacuum.
- Worker experience is two-sided: technostress carries both distress and eustress appraisals (Tronnier et al.), entry-level professionals use GenAI to craft tasks and signals (Mayer et al.), employees at a science organization worry about data, publishing norms and jobs (Wagman et al.), and alienation and expediency appear as a dark side (Hai et al., snippet).
- The IS-venue work in this scan is qualitative, single-site or conceptual (ICIS short and complete papers, ISJ practitioner case, editorial, theory paper, review of reviews), whereas the causal evidence sits in economics, management and HCI venues or in working papers. Both sides state that governance, verification and trust calibration are unresolved.

### Contradictions or tensions
- Task-level gains versus organization-level nulls. Brynjolfsson et al. report 15% more resolutions per hour and Cui et al. 26% more completed tasks, yet Humlum and Vestergaard bound earnings and hours effects at under 2% two years after ChatGPT, Bick et al. report time savings of only 1.4% of hours, and Dillon et al. find no change in the quantity or composition of work beyond individual email time. Baslandze et al. add that executives' perceived gains exceed measured gains. These are not the same outcome, but no paper read here reconciles them with data.
- Skill-leveling versus expert harm. The leveling narrative coexists, in the same papers, with small but significant quality declines for top performers (resolution rate and satisfaction in Brynjolfsson et al.; objective and subjective quality at Alibaba) and a 19% slowdown for experienced developers (METR). Brynjolfsson et al. also note that top performers supply the training examples and may under-invest in new solutions once AI echoes their own practices.
- Perceived versus measured effects. METR's developers believed they were 20% faster while being 19% slower; Baslandze et al. document a perception-measurement gap at the firm level; Schmidt et al. and the IS survey literature rely on perceived usefulness. The direction of self-report bias is therefore an open question for the survey-based IS literature.
- Version and reporting inconsistencies within sources. Dillon et al.: 56 firms and "over 6,000" workers in arXiv v1 versus 66 firms and 7,137 workers in the later abstract; Bick et al.: 40%/23%/9% in the NBER version versus 45%/27%/10% in the Management Science version (later waves); Brynjolfsson et al.: 5,179 agents and 14% in the SSRN abstract versus 5,172 and 15% in QJE; Humlum and Vestergaard's NBER paper has been retitled. Press claims attached to NBER w34984 ("6,000 executives", "89% no change") do not match the w34984 abstract and may describe a different paper.
- Trust as a determinant versus trust as irrelevant. Query 3 surfaced papers in which trust is the strongest predictor of intention to use (IJHCI snippet), while Kim and Kim find that trust does not significantly affect use once habit and perceived risk are modeled. Guardrails that raise auditability also provoke circumvention when seen as illegitimate (Leon).

### Methodological weaknesses (as visible or inferred)
- Horizons are short: weeks to months for experiments; the only multi-year outcome evidence is register-based and null.
- Single-firm or single-tool designs dominate, and the largest field experiments were run by or with the vendor (Microsoft, Alibaba, BCG, P&G); the papers disclose this but cannot remove it.
- Outcomes are activity counts, time, or graded quality on researcher-chosen tasks; organization-level value, error propagation, verification labor and the cost of circumvented governance are rarely measured. Dillon et al. observe no content; Brynjolfsson et al. have no pay data.
- "GenAI use" is operationalized inconsistently (telemetry weeks, self-reported weekly use, license access, interviews), which is the problem the ISJ editorial addresses; abstracts of the IS survey papers do not specify model, integration mode or configuration.
- Qualitative IS studies rest on 12-22 interviews at one site; the one governance quasi-experiment is single-authored and single-firm; the shadow-AI literature is conceptual, self-report-survey based or maturity-model based, with no behavioral trace data.
- The review of reviews that defines the IS agenda is itself bounded by three databases, a 2023 cut-off and a corpus weighted toward healthcare and education.

### Unresolved questions
- Where do task-level time savings go (reallocation, verification, slack, quality), and under which organizational conditions do they aggregate into measurable throughput or earnings?
- Is the expert-worker quality decline a transient learning effect, a workflow-interface problem (as Alibaba's shift-away evidence suggests), or structural?
- How can sanctioned and unsanctioned use be measured behaviorally rather than by self-report, and what determines disclosure of AI use to colleagues and managers?
- Which governance mechanisms change behavior, and when does enforcement trigger circumvention rather than compliance?
- How should "GenAI use" be specified (model, integration mode, autonomy level) so that studies are comparable, as the ISJ editorial and the IS review both demand?

## Candidate research gaps

- Micro-to-macro translation of productivity gains. Evidence: task-level gains of 12-40% (QJE, Management Science, Organization Science) coexist with precise nulls on hours and earnings (Humlum and Vestergaard), savings of about 1.4% of work hours (Bick et al.), individual email savings without organizational reallocation (Dillon et al.) and a perceived-versus-measured paradox among executives (Baslandze et al.). What would disprove it: organization-level studies linking telemetry-measured use to throughput, error rates or cycle time over a year or more that show consistent positive effects, or a visible mechanism (task reallocation, new tasks) that reconciles the two literatures quantitatively.
- Expert-worker downside and verification labor. Evidence: quality declines for top performers (Brynjolfsson et al.; Alibaba), slowdowns for experienced developers (METR), and the IS review's call to study "verification skills over creation skills". What would disprove it: field studies with experienced workers showing no quality loss once interface or workflow conditions are adjusted, or evidence that the effect disappears with newer tools.
- Behavioral measurement of shadow and undisclosed use. Evidence: DART (survey-based per snippet), the BYOAI maturity model, Schneider et al. and Cohen and Zalmanson are conceptual, survey-based or interview-based; Leon reports circumvention but as a percentage of teams; no public log-level dataset distinguishing sanctioned from unsanctioned use surfaced in this scan. What would disprove it: a published study or open dataset with trace data on sanctioned versus personal-account use inside a real organization.
- Causal evaluation of governance-of-use mechanisms. Evidence: one quasi-experiment (Leon) against many frameworks and maturity models; the IS review names adaptive governance and regulatory translation as open frontiers. What would disprove it: several field or quasi-experiments across firms showing which policy, technical and workflow controls change employee behavior and risk outcomes without large circumvention.
- Artifact under-specification in IS GenAI studies. Evidence: the ISJ editorial exists because of it; the survey papers seen here (Kim and Kim; IJHCI; PACIS teams) measure generic "GenAI use"; Dillon et al. show that the same tool is used in 21% to 75% of weeks across firms. What would disprove it: a body of IS studies that specify model, integration mode (chat, embedded copilot, agent) and configuration and show that results differ by them.
- Trust, delegation and legitimacy of controls. Evidence: contradictory roles of trust across surveys (strongest predictor versus not significant), legitimacy as a boundary condition for guardrails (Leon), and the IS review's call to theorize trust calibration and appropriate reliance. What would disprove it: convergent evidence that trust constructs predict use and compliance uniformly across tasks and control regimes.

## Limitations of this scan

- Access depth per row: 3 rows full-text (Brynjolfsson et al. arXiv v2 HTML; Dillon et al. arXiv v1 HTML, not the current v4; Jarzębowicz et al. arXiv v1 HTML, not the journal version), 11 rows abstract, 2 rows metadata/snippet (Riemer et al.; Sebastian). Supplementary items: 9 abstract, 5 metadata/snippet. Findings attributed to abstracts can omit qualifications made in the full text, and the full-text rows were read in part (abstract, design, results and conclusion sections) rather than including every appendix.
- Search engine and API coverage: 20 WebSearch queries (US-oriented engine, 4 in extended mode); domain filters leaked results from other hosts and returned nothing for ISR, MISQ, JMIS, EJIS, JAIS articles or HICSS proceedings, so those venues are under-represented here rather than shown to lack relevant work. The arXiv export API answered HTTP 429 or timed out on five of thirteen first attempts (retries succeeded); the Semantic Scholar title-search batch was abandoned after repeated 429s and only DOI/arXiv-ID lookups and citation endpoints were used; OpenAlex answered 429 throughout the verification run.
- Citation chasing: forward citations were pulled for three papers only (Brynjolfsson et al., 300 of more citations returned; Noy and Zhang, 300; Dillon et al., 28) and backward references for two (97 and 19; Semantic Scholar returned no references for the Science paper). Only titles, venues and counts were inspected; none of the citing papers was read, and the ones noted as relevant (for example "The AI Efficiency Trap", "The Complementarity Paradox", "From hidden use to accountable governance", "Build-your-own GPT", "From Exposure to Adoption: Generative AI in European Workplaces") are leads, not evidence rows.
- Sites that refused automated fetches during this run: link.springer.com (bot-protection "Client Challenge" page to curl), and, as expected, dl.acm.org, sciencedirect.com, pubsonline.informs.org, onlinelibrary.wiley.com, tandfonline.com and papers.ssrn.com were not fetched; their items were read through Crossref, Semantic Scholar or the arXiv API. The WebFetch tool was blocked by the egress proxy for arxiv.org, aisel.aisnet.org, link.springer.com and nber.org, so all page reads used curl from Bash.
- Semantic Scholar returned a mismatched abstract for Feuerriegel et al. (BISE 2024); it was discarded, and similar silent mismatches cannot be excluded for other API-sourced abstracts, although each abstract used here was checked against the title and venue.
- A sibling scout's verification job was running in the same scratch directory with the default file name; this scan therefore used prefixed file names, but the first launch (before the prefix) may have been affected and was discarded.
- Nothing here is a systematic review: no screening counts are claimed for this scan, inclusion was judgmental, and the search log records what was run, not what exists. Date of all searches, fetches and API calls: 2026-10-03.
