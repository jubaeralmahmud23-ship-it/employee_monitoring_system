# Novelty challenge: "Published but Uninformative? Sampling Uncertainty, Exclusion Rules and the Evidentiary Value of NYC Local Law 144 Impact Ratios"

Reviewer stance: adversarial (task is to refute novelty). Date of search: 2026-10-05. Idea source: `research/02_candidate_ideas_merged.json`, `ideas[3]`.

Idea in one sentence: reanalyse the public OSF corpus of NYC Local Law 144 (LL 144) bias-audit reports (449 impact-ratio rows, 80 PDFs, several with per-category applicant and selected counts) by attaching exact/bootstrap intervals, posterior probabilities of disparity and the UGESP two-standard-deviation test to each published ratio and by quantifying the populations excluded as "unknown" or under the 2% rule (Part 1); run calibrated simulations of the false-pass / false-fail rates of the LL 144 reporting rule as functions of N, category share around 2%, scoring- versus selection-rate metric, intersectional splitting and pooling (Part 2); compare reporting designs (intervals, posteriors, UGESP test, minimum-detectable-disparity statements) and manipulation sensitivity (Part 3).

## 3. Verdict: partially-covered

Reasoning (why not "likely novel"):

1. The generic statistical core is old and well documented in industrial-organizational psychology and legal statistics. Morris and Lobsenz (Personnel Psychology, 2000) already proposed a significance test on the four-fifths effect size and recommended reporting the adverse-impact ratio with a confidence interval; Roth, Bobko and Switzer (J. Applied Psychology, 2006) already ran Monte Carlo simulations showing false positives of the 4/5ths rule at realistic sample sizes and the effect of adding significance tests; Collins and Morris (2008), Switzer, Roth and Rosopa (2016), York (2002), Hauenstein et al. (2013), Gastwirth and Miao (2009) and Greenberg (1979) populate the same space. The idea's risks field anticipated this ("not searched in this scan"); the search confirms that the four-fifths-rule-under-small-samples question is a mature literature, so Part 2 cannot be presented as establishing that the rule is noisy at small N.

2. Gerchick et al. (FAccT 2025, full text read) already attach per-ratio uncertainty bounds to the published LL 144 corpus and already draw the "published but uninformative" conclusion. Their bounds come from the excluded "unknown" applicants rather than from binomial sampling, but they use exactly the published applicant and selected counts the idea plans to extract, cover 116 audits (more than the 80 PDFs in the OSF archive), find that 70% of ratios have a worst-case lower bound below 0.8 (28% under proportional allocation), and recommend uncertainty bounds as a reporting design. The idea's statement that Gerchick et al.'s corpus "is not available" is contradicted by the paper's own data statement (public GitHub repository, metadata verified). Part 1's "unknown" component is therefore largely done; what remains is the sampling component and the quantification of excluded shares.

3. The reporting designs of Part 3 are already specified by others. Lam et al. (FAccT 2024) list, as LL 144 audit criteria, uncertainty in impact-ratio calculations (Q.G), disclosure of sample size and rates for groups excluded under 2%, and UGESP significance testing with a binomial Z-test for n >= 30 and Fisher's exact test below (Q.H.1); Ferrara et al. (NeurIPS 2025) supply the Bayesian small-group posterior; Singh et al. (NeurIPS 2023 workshop) supply power-based minimum sample sizes for fairness audits; fairlearn and fairmetrics ship bootstrap intervals. The idea's Part 3 is an evaluation of existing proposals on real reports, not the design of new ones.

4. The missing "outcome evidence" exists. Aobdia, Ma and Zhang (SSRN 2025) report a difference-in-differences effect of the audit mandate on the male share of hires, and Jansen (SSRN 2026) reports null employment effects; both are working papers, but the why_matters claim that "no outcome evidence exists for audit mandates" must be dropped and the framing adjusted (audits may change behaviour even if the published numbers carry little evidence).

5. Contradicting motivation to address. Watkins, McKenna and Chen (FAccT 2024) and Raghavan and Kim (Georgetown Law Technology Review) argue that 0.8 is not the legal standard, so "statistically distinguishable from 0.8" is the wrong evidentiary target in law; the idea should present 0.8 as the convention auditors use and report distinguishability from 1.0 and from a disparity threshold as well. Gerchick et al.'s missing-data bounds will usually dwarf sampling intervals when unknowns exceed knowns (as in the BNP Paribas report's 200,486 unknowns), which means the sampling-uncertainty headline may be second-order to a problem already quantified; and vendor-level audits with tens of thousands of applicants will be precise, as the idea's risks field admits.

What was not found after 44 queries, and therefore remains the idea's claimable contribution: (a) no study attaches binomial or bootstrap sampling intervals, posterior probabilities of disparity or the UGESP test to the published LL 144 ratios using the counts actually published, nor reports the share of ratios whose interval excludes 0.8 (or 1.0) by auditor, vendor and metric type; (b) no study quantifies, in applicants, the populations removed by the 2% rule and the unknown exclusion relative to the audited populations across the corpus (Gerchick et al. report the prevalence of missingness, 83% of audits, and the groups most often dropped, not the shares); (c) no simulation of LL 144's specific discretionary choices (2% exclusion, unknown exclusion, median-split scoring rate versus selection rate, intersectional splitting, cross-employer pooling) as levers on the false-pass and false-fail rates of the reporting rule, calibrated to the empirical category-size and selection-rate distribution; (d) no "manipulation range" (how far a ratio can be moved across 0.8 by permitted choices on the same data) measured on real reports. These are an empirical reanalysis and a rule-specific simulation built on well-known statistics, not a new measurement construct; "partially-covered" is the appropriate verdict.

Recommendations if the idea proceeds: rename the contribution from "attaching uncertainty to published ratios" to "sampling and exclusion uncertainty of LL 144 ratios, extending Gerchick et al.'s missing-data bounds"; use the ACLU 116-audit dataset (after checking its licence, none shown in the repository metadata) alongside the OSF archive; cite Morris and Lobsenz (2000), Roth, Bobko and Switzer (2006), Collins and Morris (2008) and Switzer, Roth and Rosopa (2016) as the antecedents of Part 2 and read the last two in full before claiming anything about power at small N; treat Lam et al.'s Q.G/Q.H criteria and Ferrara et al.'s Dirichlet-multinomial posterior as the reporting designs being evaluated rather than proposed; cite Aobdia, Ma and Zhang and Jansen in the motivation; report distinguishability from 1.0 as well as from 0.8 in light of Watkins et al. and Raghavan and Kim; pre-register the decision rule for which of Gerchick et al.'s bounds and the sampling intervals dominates per report.
