## 2. Labels and measurements (adequacy for the intended claim)

- **The outcome variable of Part 1 is a count pair (N applicants, n selected or above median) per category per audit.** The OSF CSV does not carry it (verified today: columns listed above, ratios only). Three sources do: (a) the PDFs in `Employer Documents.zip` (the idea record reports that pdftotext yielded tables for Bloomberg, BNP Paribas and Eightfold and nothing for Wells Fargo and Lumen); (b) Gerchick et al.'s `audits_full.csv`, which has already extracted `number_of_applications` and `n_selected_or_n_above_median` for 116 audits in 44 reports, including the `Missing/Unknown` rows that give the excluded-unknown counts; (c) the 2025–2026 reports linked from the ACLU tracker, of which the one sample read today (SC/SHL June 2026) carries `# of Applicants` per group and the unknown count. **Recommendation:** make `audits_full.csv` the primary Part 1 corpus, use the OSF PDFs as an independent extraction check on the 13–17 reports both corpora share, and add 2025–2026 reports from the tracker as an extension sample. This removes OCR from the critical path.
- **How many ratios will carry usable counts?** Not countable without downloading the 2.3 MB file. Indirect evidence: the DCWP rule *requires* "the number of applicants in a category and scoring rate of a category" in the summary of results, Gerchick et al. describe the counts as "often reported in tables", and their Figures 2–3 compute bounds for every audit "that disclose[s] the number of applications missing demographic information". Their caveat is that `result` is "not always" equal to n/N as reported, so a preregistered consistency rule (|n/N − result| ≤ rounding tolerance; else flag) is needed, and 54 % of audits contain at least one ratio above 1 because the comparator group varied, so the comparator must be recomputed from counts rather than taken from the report.
- **Duplicates and pooling.** Wright's CSV has a `DuplicateAuditReport` flag; Gerchick et al. document "silent duplicates" (identical results across employers' reports of the same vendor tool, all by one auditor, almost all HireVue). The unit of analysis "impact ratio (category × audit)" must therefore be defined after de-duplication at the level of (auditor, tool, data window), otherwise the share of "indistinguishable from 0.8" ratios is inflated by repeated rows.
- **OCR.** `tesseract` is not on this machine's PATH (checked); `pytesseract` 0.3.13 (Apache-2.0) and `ocrmypdf` 17.13.0 (MPL-2.0, requires Python ≥3.11) are pure-Python wrappers on PyPI and both need the Tesseract binary (the `UB-Mannheim/tesseract` Windows installer repository is active, last pushed 2026-07-29; `tesseract-ocr/tesseract` is Apache-2.0); ocrmypdf additionally wants Ghostscript (`gs` absent). `PyMuPDF` 1.27.2.3, `pdfplumber` 0.11.10, `pypdf` 6.14.2 and `pdfminer.six` are already installed, and `pdftotext` (poppler, via Git Bash's mingw64) is present. For two scanned reports, manual double-entry transcription (an hour or two) is cheaper and more auditable than an OCR pipeline; with `audits_full.csv` available it may be unnecessary.
- **Statistics are standard and need no labels beyond the counts.** Exact and bootstrap intervals for a ratio of binomial proportions, the Morris–Lobsenz interval, Fisher's exact test, the UGESP two-standard-deviation (pooled z) test, and a Beta-posterior P(ratio < 0.8) are all closed-form or Monte Carlo on two count pairs. `fairlearn.metrics.demographic_parity_ratio` (ratio of smallest to largest group selection rate; `MetricFrame.ratio()` for the computation) exists and reports no intervals, which is precisely the baseline the idea criticises; `aequitas` 1.1.0 exposes Bias/Fairness classes with disparity ratios but its PyPI metadata says `requires-python <3.14, >=3.10`, so it would need the Python 3.12 interpreter that is also installed (`py -0` lists 3.12 and 3.14). Neither library is required; a 100-line numpy implementation is more transparent and was benchmarked below.
- **Measurement precision for the primary quantity** ("proportion of published ratios whose 95 % interval excludes 0.8") is fine: with several hundred de-duplicated ratios the proportion has a half-width of ±3–5 points, and the corpus is the population of published audits, so the honest frame is descriptive with finite-population caveats rather than inferential.
- **Scoring-rate audits.** For tools audited by median split, the "selected" count is the number above the sample median; the sampling model is a hypergeometric-like two-sample comparison rather than independent binomials. The simulation (Part 2) already generates scores and splits at the median, so the empirical and simulated quantities match; this should be stated.

Installed on 3.14 (pip list): numpy 2.4.6, scipy 1.17.1, pandas 3.0.5, scikit-learn 1.9.0, statsmodels 0.14.6, matplotlib 3.11.0, pdfplumber, PyMuPDF, pypdf, pdfminer.six, and torch 2.12.0 (**CPU build**: `torch.cuda.is_available()` is False, CUDA None). PyPI wheels checked: numpy 2.5.3, scipy 1.18.1, pandas 3.0.6, scikit-learn 1.9.1 all list cp312/cp313 win_amd64 wheels in their current release (cp314 wheels were not in the first 14 tags printed, but the 3.14 interpreter already has working builds of all four); folktables 0.0.12 and fairlearn 0.14.0 are pure Python.

**Micro-benchmark run today (numpy/scipy, single process; an assessment-time scratch measurement made to ground the compute estimates, not a study result; the throwaway script is not part of the records and the figures below, including the by-product rates, must not be cited as findings):**
- Part 1 style: 449 ratios × (10,000 bootstrap draws + 20,000 posterior draws + pooled-z + Fisher exact) = **1.34 s**.
- Part 2 style, selection-rate tool: one simulation cell = 10,000 replicate audits with N = 2,000 applicants in 6 categories, 2 % exclusion applied, impact ratios and false-pass flags computed vectorised = **8.9 ms**. (As a by-product the cell showed the mechanism the idea is after: a category with true share 2 % was excluded in 48 % of replicates and, when a true ratio of 0.76 was induced, published a ratio ≥ 0.8 in 19.8 % of replicates.)
- Part 2 style, scoring-rate tool: 1,000 replicate audits with continuous scores and a median split, unvectorised inner loop = **0.19 s**.

**Estimated CPU hours.**
- *Pilot* (Part 1 on `audits_full.csv` plus 3 OSF PDFs; Part 2 on a 3 × 3 × 2 grid with 2,000 replicates): computation under a minute; the pilot's time is human extraction and checking, roughly 2–3 working days.
- *Full study.* Part 1: seconds. Part 2 as written: say 12 sample sizes × 10 category shares × 8 true ratios × 2 metrics × 2 (marginal/intersectional) × 5 pooling schemes = 19,200 cells × 10,000 replicates. Selection-rate cells ≈ 9,600 × 0.009 s ≈ 1.5 min; scoring-rate cells ≈ 9,600 × ~2 s (unvectorised; ~10× less if vectorised) ≈ 5 CPU-hours; score models fitted once per calibration on an ACS state file with scikit-learn logistic regression or gradient boosting: minutes. Part 3 (manipulation sensitivity: enumerate permitted choices per real audit) is a few thousand recomputations per audit: minutes. Monte Carlo error at 10,000 replicates is ±0.2 pp for a 5 % rate, adequate; 100,000 replicates for the headline cells costs 10× on those cells only.
  - **Scenario A (4 cores, 15 GB, no GPU):** full study ≈ 5–15 CPU-hours worst case (older cores run perhaps 2–3× slower per core than the i7-14700KF measured here), i.e. an overnight run even single-threaded; RAM peak well under 2 GB; disk < 1 GB including ACS PUMS state files (the NY person file is tens of MB) and the OSF zip. **Compute is not a constraint in scenario A.**
  - **Scenario B (20 cores / 28 threads, 32 GB, RTX 5070 Ti):** the same grid parallelised with `multiprocessing` or joblib across 20 cores finishes in roughly 15–45 minutes wall-clock; the GPU is unused. Python 3.14 is fine for everything in the plan except aequitas (needs 3.12); torch is not needed, so the usual "torch lags on 3.14" problem does not arise. If folktables or fairlearn fail to import on 3.14, the 3.12 interpreter is the fallback and nothing in the design changes.

**Does the core method need an LLM or embedding model?** No. Every statistic is a function of count pairs; the simulation is binomial/multinomial sampling plus scikit-learn classifiers for score generation. The only step where an LLM is tempting is **table extraction from heterogeneous PDFs**. The non-LLM alternative is established and already used by the closest prior work: Gerchick et al. extracted their 116 audits with `tabulapdf`/`tabulizer` in R plus manual entry and a full manual validation pass, and release the extraction template and helper functions. In Python the equivalents are `pdfplumber.extract_tables()` and `camelot-py` 2.0.0 (MIT, pure Python; lattice/stream modes), both reachable on PyPI, with `pdftotext -layout` as the fallback that the idea record already used. Because the corpus is small (tens of reports) and every number must be hand-verified anyway, deterministic extraction plus double entry is both cheaper and more defensible than a model, and keeps `runs_without_llm: true` honest. No paid API is needed anywhere.

## 4. Approvals and ethics

- **No human participants, surveys, interviews or private data.** Inputs are (i) legally mandated public disclosures by employers and vendors, (ii) two research datasets derived from them (OSF CC BY 4.0; GitHub, licence not shown), (iii) US Census public-use microdata and public benchmark tables. **No IRB/ethics review is required**; a departmental "not human-subjects research" determination is advisable only because employers and auditors are named.
- **Naming employers and auditors.** The idea necessarily reports results "by auditor, vendor and metric type". Wright et al., Gerchick et al. and the ACLU tracker already name every entity, and the law's purpose is public scrutiny of these documents, so naming is proportionate. Two cautions: (a) a wide interval around a published ratio is a statement about *sample size*, not about the employer's hiring, and the write-up must say so each time a name appears; (b) where the project finds arithmetic inconsistencies (Gerchick et al. report rates not equal to n/N) it should report them as "as published" discrepancies without attributing intent, and consider giving named auditors an opportunity to comment before publication (a courtesy, not a requirement).
- **Secondary use of public documents.** Quoting aggregate counts and ratios from audit summaries is scholarly fair use; the documents contain no individual-level data. Reports that employers later removed (the tracker notes "Removed from employer website" for several) are preserved on Perma.cc; using archived copies is standard practice (Gerchick et al. cite Perma links throughout) but Perma.cc blocks scripted access, so retrieval is manual.
- **Licence hygiene.** Release derived tables under CC BY 4.0 with attribution to Wright et al.; for `audits_full.csv`, publish code that reads the file from GitHub and derived statistics only, unless the ACLU team grants a licence. Census PUMS carries no restriction; cite the ACS vintage.
- **Preregistration** (the idea calls Parts 1 and 2 confirmatory) can be filed on OSF with the exact de-duplication rule, consistency filter, interval method and the 0.8 convention fixed in advance.

## 5. What the design can and cannot claim

**Can claim (internally valid):**
- For every published ratio with usable counts: an exact or bootstrap 95 % interval, a posterior probability of a disparity below 0.8, a UGESP two-SD verdict and a Fisher p-value, and therefore the **share of published LL 144 ratios that are statistically distinguishable from 0.8 at the sample sizes auditors actually used**, overall and by auditor, vendor and metric type. This is a descriptive property of the published corpus and is fully supportable.
- The **size of the excluded populations**: unknown-category counts (required by rule; present in Gerchick's schema as `Missing/Unknown` rows and in the 2026 sample report) and sub-2 % categories (the rule requires their N and rate to be printed even when excluded), relative to the audited N. Gerchick et al. already report that 83 % of audits had missing demographic data and compute worst-case and proportional bounds on the ratios (70 % and 28 % of ratios could fall below 0.8 under their two assumptions). The idea's unknown-share analysis is therefore a **replication and extension**, not a first finding, and must be framed as such; the sampling-uncertainty layer and the simulation are the new parts.
- **Simulation claims** about the LL 144 reporting rule as a procedure: false-pass and false-fail rates as functions of N, category share around 2 %, selection- vs scoring-rate metric, intersectional splitting and pooling across heterogeneous employers, with Monte Carlo error. These are claims about the rule, calibrated to the observed distribution of N and shares, and are supportable. They extend Roth et al. (2006), who simulated the 4/5ths rule's false positives generically, by adding the LL 144-specific exclusion, median-split and pooling mechanics; the paper must cite Roth et al. and Morris–Lobsenz and present Part 2 as rule-specific, not as a discovery that ratios are noisy in small samples.
- **Reporting-design comparison** (intervals, posterior statements, two-SD test, minimum detectable disparity) evaluated on the real sample sizes: supportable as a decision-analytic comparison.

**Cannot claim (or must be scoped):**
- Anything about the **true disparity of any tool or employer**. Published audits are a self-selected sample (Wright et al.: 18 of 391 employers posted; auditors in Groves et al. said many clients did not post), ratios are reported after discretionary choices, and the pooled vendor audits with hundreds of thousands of applicants will be precise while small employer audits will not. The honest headline is conditional: "among published audits, X % of employer-level ratios are compatible with both compliance and substantial disparity".
- **Causal effects of the law** on hiring or on audit practice; the design is observational and descriptive. The 2023→2024 companion pairs (13 in Gerchick et al.) and 2025–2026 tracker entries allow a descriptive time series only.
- **Legal conclusions.** The rule's "statistically significant bias audit" phrase is undefined; the paper can show what different statistical standards would have changed, not what the law requires.
- **Generalisation to the whole LL 144 universe** or to other jurisdictions; and no claim that an LLM-free extraction is error-free: report inter-extractor agreement against Gerchick's values for the shared reports.
- **If** nearly all usable ratios turn out precise (large vendor pools dominate), the sampling-uncertainty headline fails and the contribution shifts to exclusion, pooling and manipulation sensitivity, as the idea record already anticipates; the preregistration should name this branch.

## 6. Blockers

1. **Licence of `audits_full.csv` not shown.** The most efficient Part 1 corpus has no LICENSE file. Mitigation: use it as input and release derived statistics plus a loader script; or email the ACLU analytics contact for permission. Not a hard blocker; the OSF corpus (CC BY 4.0) plus own extraction is the fallback.
2. **Prior art reframes two of three parts.** Morris and Lobsenz (2000) give intervals and tests for the adverse-impact ratio; Roth, Bobko and Switzer (2006) simulate the 4/5ths rule's small-sample behaviour; Gerchick et al. (2025) already bound ratios under missing-data assumptions and quantify missingness; Raghavan and Kim argue the point legally. The idea's `undermining_evidence` field asked exactly these questions; the answers are "yes, partly". The contribution must rest on (a) sampling intervals attached to the real LL 144 corpus, (b) rule-specific simulation (2 % exclusion, unknown exclusion, median split, pooling, intersectional splitting), (c) manipulation sensitivity on real reports, and (d) the reporting-design comparison. This is a positioning change, not a feasibility problem.
3. **Data-quality rules must be fixed before analysis**: de-duplication of silent duplicates and multi-threshold rows, a consistency filter for rate ≠ n/N, comparator recomputation (54 % of audits have a ratio above 1), and handling of scoring-rate (median-split) audits with a matching sampling model.
4. **Automated access is blocked for several sources**: nyc.gov (FAQ, programme page), osc.ny.gov (Comptroller audit), perma.cc (archived reports), a860-gpp.nyc.gov. Each is reachable in a normal browser; the DCWP adopted rule itself is reachable and was read, which removes the idea record's main documentary gap.
5. **OCR toolchain absent** (no tesseract binary, no Ghostscript); needed only if the two scanned OSF reports are extracted by machine. Manual transcription or Gerchick's extracted values suffice.
6. **`Employer Documents.zip` contents not re-verified today** (80 PDFs per the idea record from the 2026-10-03 download on the cloud machine); the size and name on OSF match the record.
7. Minor: aequitas does not support Python 3.14 (use 3.12 if wanted); folktables/fairlearn on 3.14 unverified; arXiv export API returned empty bodies to id_list queries today (abstract pages worked).

None is a resource blocker; 1–3 are decisions to make before preregistration, 4–7 are setup details.

## 7. Verdict

**feasible-now.**

Reasoning: every input exists, is public and is reachable today — the OSF archive (CC BY 4.0; file list, sizes and licence confirmed via the API), Gerchick et al.'s machine-readable dataset of 116 audits with per-group applicant and selected counts (GitHub tree and schema read), the ACLU tracker with 98 report links including 2025–2026 entries, the DCWP adopted rule text (read), census PUMS, UCI Adult and OpenML 1590 (all 200). The 2026 sample report confirms that current reports still print per-category counts and the unknown-category count. The computation is trivial: the entire confirmatory programme was benchmarked at seconds for Part 1 and a few CPU-hours for Part 2 even on scenario A's 4 cores; scenario B finishes in under an hour and its GPU is irrelevant. No LLM, embedding model, paid API, participant or approval is needed; table extraction has a documented non-LLM route that the closest prior work itself used. The caveats are scientific rather than logistical: the statistical tools and the generic small-sample result are prior art (2000, 2006), Gerchick et al. have already bounded the missing-data effect, and the corpus is self-selected, so the paper must be positioned as a rule-specific reanalysis and simulation of published audits. The one administrative loose end is the unstated licence of `audits_full.csv`, which has a clean fallback.

## 8. Minimum change in resources that would unblock the full design
