# Novelty challenge: "Documentation as Audit Evidence: How Often Do Self-Reported Model-Card Benchmark Scores Agree with Independent Evaluations of the Same Checkpoint?"

Reviewer stance: adversarial (task is to refute novelty). Date of search: 2026-10-05. Idea source: `research/02_candidate_ideas_merged.json`, `ideas[6]`.

Idea in one sentence: for every Hugging Face model that the Open LLM Leaderboard (v1 and v2) re-evaluated with a fixed harness, parse the author-written benchmark claims in the model card (markdown tables and inline statements, separated from bot-populated `model-index` entries), score each claim's verifiability (benchmark, metric, n-shot/config, harness or version, checkpoint identity, source), and, for claims on a benchmark the leaderboard also ran on the same checkpoint sha, express self-reported minus independent score in units of the leaderboard's standard error, against a noise floor from models with two independent result files; then regress discrepancy and verifiability on organisation type, downloads, licence, fine-tune/merge status, release date and presence of a bot-populated model-index.

## 3. Verdict: partially-covered (close to largely-covered)

Reasoning (why not "likely novel", and why not quite "largely-covered"):

1. The headline construct has been published. Kadasi et al. (ICWSM 2025) compared author-reported model-card scores with their own independent evaluation of 500 Hugging Face models and reported that about 88% of authors overstate performance. That is "self-reported model-card scores vs independent re-evaluation of the same uploaded model, on Hugging Face, at platform scale for one task", and it is the sentence a reviewer will write first. The idea's text does not cite it. The grep shows the ICWSM comparison is loose (reported numbers may refer to other datasets; no checkpoint identity; no uncertainty), so the idea's benchmark- and sha-matched, stderr-normalised version is a real methodological improvement, but it is an improvement on an existing result, not a new question.
2. The two measurement objects already run as deployed signals. Evaluation Cards (EvalEval Coalition, 2026; Hugging Face and Stanford authors) computes a reproducibility/completeness signal (the idea's verifiability score: n-shot, prompt template, generation args, with first-party rows populating 0.0% of base reproducibility fields vs 16.6% for third-party rows) and a cross-party divergence flag (the idea's discrepancy: 7.2% of multi-party (model, benchmark) pairs and 51.9% of 181 multi-organisation metric groups diverge by more than 5%), over 5,816 models and roughly 102K results that include Open LLM Leaderboard v2 via Every Eval Ever. Its own limitation note ("uniform threshold; ignores sampling variance") is exactly the gap the idea's stderr-normalised statistic and noise floor would fill, which makes the idea read as the statistical follow-up to Evaluation Cards rather than a new study. The idea's `difference` field describes Evaluation Cards as merely "propos[ing] a reporting layer"; the full text shows it also reports the empirical divergence numbers.
3. The leaderboard data have already been analysed at scale with the right statistics. Yang and Chen (2026) audit 394 adjacent-rank claims on the Open LLM Leaderboard at item level and find 391 unsupported (median z = 0.17); Federiakin (2025) re-ranks v1 and v2 psychometrically; Dekoninck et al. find the top-3 leaderboard models contaminated. The idea's "noise floor from models with two result files" is a weaker version of the item-level resampling already done; and contamination (ConStat, Benchmark Inflation) is a competing explanation for any positive discrepancy that the design does not separate from misreporting.
4. The leaderboard operators themselves documented the main validity threat in 2023: the same llama-65b checkpoint scores 0.488 or 0.636 on MMLU depending on implementation, and discussions #829 and #424 show the leaderboard's own numbers can be mis-configured by third-party submitters (chat template, `few-shot-as-multiturn`). A card-vs-harness gap is therefore an implementation difference by default. The idea acknowledges this and proposes to stratify by configuration completeness, but Evaluation Cards' finding that first-party reports populate 0.0% of base reproducibility fields, together with Kadasi et al.'s 80% of cards lacking evaluation detail, predicts that the configuration-matched subset on which "misstatement" can be asserted will be very small; the realistic deliverable is RQ1's verifiability profile, which Evaluation Cards already reports.
5. The governance argument is already at FAccT 2026 twice (Baack et al. on selective benchmark highlighting as marketing; Kuehnert et al. on vendor self-reports failing as standalone evaluation evidence), and the IS framing (independent verification as the discipline on voluntary disclosure) is standard in accounting since at least Ball et al. (2012). The idea would be a quantitative instance of an established argument.

What was not found after 42 queries, and therefore remains the idea's claimable contribution: no study that (a) matches author-typed model-card benchmark claims to an independent harness run of the identical checkpoint sha on the same benchmark, (b) expresses the gap in units of the independent run's standard error against a repeated-run noise floor, (c) separates author-typed claims from bot-populated `model-index` entries, and (d) models discrepancy and verifiability as a function of uploader type, merge status, downloads and licence on the full v1 plus v2 population of roughly 16K evaluated models. The verifiability coding scheme would be new only as a per-claim audit instrument; its fields duplicate Evaluation Cards' schema. These are protocol-level and statistical increments on Kadasi et al. and Evaluation Cards, not a new construct; "partially-covered" is the appropriate verdict, and a reviewer could defensibly argue "largely-covered".

Recommendations if the idea proceeds: cite and position against Kadasi et al. (ICWSM 2025) and Evaluation Cards (2026) in the first paragraph, and frame the work as "a checkpoint-matched statistical replication of the overstatement finding on LLMs"; reuse ModelTables (card tables) and Every Eval Ever (leaderboard v2 converter) instead of new regex extraction, and report what they miss; make the primary outcome the share of card-vs-leaderboard gaps that survive configuration matching, with the MMLU 2023 case as the anchor; add a contamination covariate (ConStat-style or the leaderboard's Flagged field) so that positive discrepancies are not read as misreporting by default; pre-register a minimum sample size for RQ2 and the fallback to RQ1/RQ3 if fewer than a few hundred configuration-matched claims exist; read the full texts of Kadasi et al. (Section 4.2 and Figure 6) and Evaluation Cards (Sections 4.2 and H.1.4) before committing, since this review only grepped them.
